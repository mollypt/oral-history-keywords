AL: What was, what would you, how would you describe the ethnic makeup of the school?
YK: Well, eighty percent Japanese, and the rest Hispanics. Because as the Japanese moved in, the neighbors, Hispanics, moved out. I don't know, they must have come to East L.A. Then there were a few Chinese, few Indians, very few.
AL: Indians like Native Americans, or people from India?
YK: Native American. Because when the Olivers started our clubhouse, was instrumental in teaching American Indians, Chinese, Japanese, Spanish, and a few Russians that immigrated. They were teaching them how to cook American-style in our institute, Stimson Institute, the name of the building that took over our Japanese school.
AL: Who ran that?
YK: Well, it was the Stimson Institute. I don't know what was their, what they did, but the name I remember from the title on the... Mrs. Bleaker was the name of the caretaker.
AL: Is that where the, Olivers clubhouse was, the Stimson Institute?
YK: Yes, that was the second floor. We took over the second floor of the institute. The Japanese school became the first floor.
AL: Is that the Daiichi gakuin?
YK: Yes, yes.
AL: Did you go to Japanese school?
YK: Uh-huh.
AL: For how many years?
YK: Oh, God... we did enroll in Japanese school, but as we got into the higher grades, we were always playing hooky because high school after-school program interfered with our Japanese school. So I don't know, the girls all went to school, they didn't play hooky, I know, but we did. So we had a time trying to get by playing hooky and then not getting caught playing hooky.
AL: Who would catch you, your parents or was there, was there a Japanese school truant warden?
YK: Well, there was a truant officer, he was an older fellow, and he was a bus driver, too, so he knew everybody.
AL: What did you, what kind of things did you study in Japanese school? Like when you went to class, what was it like?
YK: It was mostly -- excuse me -- mostly fairy tales.
AL: Do you remember any of 'em?
YK: Well, the one I remember mostly is "Momotaro." Did, did you know the story?
AL: Tell me.
YK: Momotaro... but incidentally, that came from the prefecture where my folks came from, Okayama. He was a, well, the folks, his, Momotaro's folks, didn't have a child. And somehow, Momotaro came rolling down the river, river, and he was a son of a peach, I guess. [Laughs]
AL: [Laughs] "Son of a peach?"
YK: Yes.
AL: 'Cause that sounds like something else.
YK: Yeah. Well, so he became the adopted son of the parents who didn't have a son. I think he rided along, I guess like Saint George, you know. Like most fairy tale, they write that all from, I don't know, some ogre or dragon that comes around to terrify the villagers. So I'm not on, too much on Japanese fairy tales.
