TO: Let me   go to high school now.
AL: Okay.   You graduate on to high school.
TO: Yeah,   and I was talking about, how institutions tend to put young people   into tracks. My sister studied secretarial sciences, although she   was a very bright person, at Roosevelt High School, in East Los   Angeles. And when I went to Roosevelt, here I'm eight years   younger, and the same thing: guidance counselors see me, and they   said, "Your people make good secretaries," and put me in   secretarial science. And I was always a mouth, and I told the   counselor, "Me? Sit still and take orders?" And... but they put me   in clerical anyway. So I took typing, bookkeeping, and didn't do   very well. But I did well in English, and did well in all the other   general courses and all, and that school had such a poor curriculum   that you could major in math/science and still finish everything   before the twelfth grade, because they really didn't have much   going for them. I remember my senior year, I think we had senior   problems, gym, maybe one class of English, and it was just   terrible. But anyway, I told them, "About ready to graduate, I'm   going to UCLA." And they said, "Oh, no, you can't keep... compete   with kids from the West Side." And I said, "Whose fault is that?"   And they said, "Well, you don't have all the courses, you can't get   in," and whatever else and all that. I said, "Not my problem." Well   anyway, so I had to go to summer school and to junior college in   order to kind of catch up, because the schools... even if you were   an academic student at your school in East L.A., you didn't have   all the prerequisites to go to UCLA. So I had to take them in   junior college, and finally go to UCLA. And I've always had a kind   of a resentment about the differences in educational opportunities.   But I did graduate UCLA, and I was offered wonderful positions on   the West Side, in Brentwood, at what were considered demonstration   schools, but I said no, and I taught at Uni High School, which was   then a very fine public high school in the West Side. But I said,   "No, I'm going to East L.A." So I first taught in Montebello for   couple of years, and then I moved over to Stevenson Junior High   School, where I'd attended, which is across the street from where I   grew up.
AL: A full   circle.
TO: And   what happened during that period, is, I don't know if you ever   heard of the Brownouts, but this is when the Brown Berets and   others were organizing, and young people were walking out of   schools, protesting the quality or the lack of quality of   educational offerings. So I remember, I had already gotten my   master's degree, and I was about ready to get my, either Ph.D. or   administrative credential, and I decided, "No, I'm going to go to   law school," and I wanted to focus on education. So I went to   Loyola Law School and I received a Reginald Huber Smith fellowship,   it was for poverty lawyers. And I was assigned to Western Center on   Law and Poverty. Western Center is a public-interest, law-reform   firm, and I just got out of law school in June, took the bar in   August, and I was a trainee in September, and they were prepared to   go to trial on a major landmark case: Serrano vs. Priest .   The nature of the case was challenging the state system of school   finance. The argument is because it's based on property taxes, it   results in disparities.
AL: Was   this the Supreme Court, or state court?
TO: Yes,   Supreme Court. And so the case had already been heard by the   Supreme Court on the law, and now we were going to go to trial in   December, and I show up at Western Center sometime in September. So   Judge, now Judge Terry Hatter, the director, said, "Well, Rose is a   former teacher, so let's have her help on trial preparation. So I   did that, and spent two years of my life, it was really, very, very   long, long hours, but a wonderful victory. And it's an interesting   story, I just told a little snippet of it recently at the memorial   service for the judge that presided.
AL: What   year is this?
TO: This   is '72, the trial started in '72, and it was really a David and   Goliath kind of a situation. Here you had Beverly Hills with the   school system, and Beverly Hills High School represented by the top   law firms in the city, and the County Council's office, and the   Attorney General's office, and on this side, we had a young man   named John McDermitt, who came from a big firm, but he only had   about a couple of trials, and myself, who just got out of law   school. There was another guy, Sid Rowlinsky, who was on the case,   but he left after a month, but the trial lasted six months. And the   first month is the easy part, where you're just kind of putting in   your direct, and now this time.
I'll tell   you one story: this, this magnificent judge, an African American,   had written a bench book and it the Treatise on Evidence,   and so we kind of put that on our counsel table to impress him.   Anyway, one day at trial, we had our witness, a school finance   expert, on the stand, and so John, here we'd been abandoned by Sid,   a trial lawyer, so now John had this whole yellow legal pad full of   questions, and he would ask the question, and then there would be a   chorus from all these lawyers on the left, saying, "Objection." And   then the judge would sustain it, and so John went through all these   questions in his legal pad, and then it became very quiet and I   looked up and I saw the judge looking at me, the court reporter   looking at me, and the bailiff looking at me. I turned and opposing   counsel all looking over this way, so I turned to the right, and   there's John, laying prostrate on the table. Here I am -- this had   to be January, I just got my bar results in December, I'd never   tried a case. And I don't know what came over me, but I leaned over   and I whispered in his ear, I said, "John, ask a hypothetical   question." And John said, "Suppose..." and he went back to the top   of his list, and he asked the question. It got in, and he went   through the entire pad. We got our case in, we prevailed, and to   this day, I don't know where that came from. And I recently said at   this judge's memorial, I says, "You know what? Possibly I had my   hands on his book, and it came up through osmosis." [Laughs]
AL: That's   interesting.
TO: I   says, "The judge was looking at me, and it was mental telepathy,"   or I said, "Or maybe it was just divine intervention." But anyway,   no, that's just a little diversion there.
AL: Did   you win the case?
TO: We won   the case, but how I ended up in City Hall, when we won the case,   the Center still had to go to the state legislature in order to   pass enabling legislation. They won in the courts, but now -- so I   decided to go work for Mayor Bradley to learn how to make laws, and   I became a legislative research coordinator. And I worked in a lot   of state and federal... the legislative analysis, and advocacy for   the city.
AL: That's   cool.
