AD: How did you perceive yourself as a Japanese American?
PC: I don't think we really noticed, or at least I didn't. Maybe I'm more dense than others, but I don't think I really noticed that I was different in that way growing up. We were part of the community, we went to a small community church, Mom sang in the choir, we were invited to people's homes, participated with our neighbors, and I don't think it ever occurred to me that I was different because I was Japanese until I was in college, when we started talking about racial differences, and then you started noticing certain things. When I was growing up, if I think somebody didn't like me, I figured it was because I was a girl or I wore glasses or was overweight or whatever. It didn't ever occur to be that maybe somebody didn't like me because of a racial issue.
AD: How... contrast that, I guess, with your mom's experience. How does that make you feel that you grew up feeling...
PC: We grew up very protected. I know at one time I was talking to Mom about why she didn't talk about some of her experiences and things, and I remember that she said that one of the things they didn't want us to do, she and Dad didn't want my sisters and I to do was to grow up with any hard feelings towards anybody else or having any prejudice for maybe what happened to them. She didn't want the negative things to affect us. And so in that way, I'm very grateful because it gave us a chance to find out what happened when we were old enough to understand it better.
AD: When you think about, sort of, that issue, the legacy, why is it important for you and for your kids, or just those generations... I guess when you think about the generational impact, why do you think it's important for kids today, or any of the generations that came after your parents to know what happened? Why is it important for us to be educated?
PC: At any time, when people panic or get frightened, and they're looking for somebody to scapegoat, you can turn on anyone. And so what happened then can happen again, whether it's against Arab Americans or whoever. You have to remember not to follow the group and not always do something out of fear. You need to think about it. And also to remember that our rights are very precious, and you have to look after them and stand up for them. And I think in this day and age, we have more opportunity, more political clout, so to speak, so that if something happens, there's more of an opportunity to make ourselves heard. Whereas for them, theNisei, they were the first ones to be born here. There wasn't anything in place for them to be able to pull on to support, to draw together and have any power against the panic and the hysteria.
AD: Tell us, even apart from the politics, tell us about your relationship with your mom.
PC: She's pretty special. I really think she's got a lot on the ball. She's taught us a lot about, in education, just in her involvement in the community, whether it was in the church or in the PTA or on the school board. Emphasis on education and her willingness to be active and support the schools. And I think I just always admired her, that she was able to do that and be so involved and become chairman of the school board. So that when my older sister graduated from high school, she was the one handing out the diplomas.
AD: That is amazing.
