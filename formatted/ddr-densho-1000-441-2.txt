TI: So tell me a little bit about Portland. What kind of house or place did you stay in?
LH: Okay, my parents owned this cleaning plant, it was a retail/wholesale plant, and our house was actually... it would be like a split level, our house was above it but to the side of it, and then, so we lived upstairs, but not directly above the shop.
TI: I just recognized, you said you were born in 1929, your dad moved there in 1928?
LH: No, he moved... oh, that's right, he moved when Tom was a year old.
TI: So that'd be about 1928.
LH: Yeah, right, right.
TI: But the question I was going to ask is, that's right about the beginning of the Depression.
LH: Right.
TI: And so you were just a baby when it happened, but how did the family sort of survive?
LH: Survive during the Depression? You know, I wasn't aware of a depression, and the shop managed and he did okay without going into debt, so he was very proud of that. And I don't know, we were all poor, right? But I wasn't aware that we were poor, so that's sort of nice. [Laughs]
TI: But at least he had a place to stay, a business that would, I guess, eke out enough money, that he could do that.
LH: Yeah, he was doing okay, he had a car, he used to deliver. What he used to do is, in addition to having a retail business, people would come in off the street. But we did a wholesale business, so he would go to these little shops in Japantown, pick up the cleaning from these different, I imagine they were laundry shops, and he picked them up and he'd bring them home, clean them, and deliver. So sometimes I would go on the delivery.
TI: And describe one of those deliveries.
LH: Well, I'm just in the car. [Laughs] But he'd either pick up clothing or return the clothing to that shop that's all cleaned and pressed.
TI: And were the shops that he would visit, were they...
LH: Japanese owned.
TI: ...Japanese owned? Okay. Yeah, I never heard of that kind of business, both the wholesale... lot of times I hear of the Japanese, but they would do the cleaning themselves, but then they would actually, your dad would pick it up and do actually most of the work, right?
LH: Right, all the work. He and my mother, they worked together in the shop.
TI: And so at night they would be busy.
LH: Frequently I remember looking down and saying we were hungry, it was time for dinner, but they were busy. So that used to make my mother feel bad.
TI: And at some point, did your brother start helping out?
LH: My older brother George did. He's the only one that worked in the shop.
TI: Where was the shop located?
LH: Located? It would be Northeast Portland, it was on Union and Stanton, and at that time, it was an all-Caucasian neighborhood, but now it's not, it's in an integrated area.
TI: And when you say all Caucasians, were there other ethnic groups in terms of, like...
LH: No, just our family and about two or three blocks away, another Japanese family. And that's pretty much it.
TI: So when you started going to school, then you were about the only Japanese?
LH: In my class, right.
