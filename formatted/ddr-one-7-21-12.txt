[This transcript is a translation of the original Japanese text.]
BB: I am sorry that one of your children passed away.
MO: Yes.
BB: How did she die? Was it Yoshiko?
MO: She suffered from aneurysm. Brain aneurysm. The timing was unfortunate because that was when I developed aneurysm and had an operation. It was in May. I was discharged on the 18th. I came home on the 18th, and Yoshiko was there. She picked me up. We came home and had lunch together. I got a phone call on Sunday evening and was told that she was dead. That was two days after. I was stunned. We had the same thing. I was older, but early detection saved my life. My daughter had brain aneurysm, and it just ruptured. That was the end.
BB: She died young.
MO: Yes, she was only fifty-eight. I am very sorry for her.
BB: You told me that Noriko does not have clear recollections about the war.
MO: It doesn't look like she remembers about it very well. She says she doesn't remember when I ask her.
BB: How do you think she was feeling back then? Do you think Noriko was afraid?
MO: I think she was, but she was only a kindergartener. Children were wearing a folded cushion on top of their head, and have you heard of air-raid shelters? Kids went school with a folded cushion tied on the head. She was still small and does not seem to remember a lot. I ask her, and she says she doesn't remember.
BB: Koichi was born before, after the war.
MO: He was born after the war. He doesn't know anything about it. He doesn't even speak Japanese. [Laughs]
BB: Does he speak any Japanese now?
MO: Not very well. [Laughs]
BB: I see.
MO: I speak Japanese to him, and he speaks English back to me. He doesn't really speak Japanese. He understands Okayama dialect though.
BB: Does Noriko speak Japanese?
MO: Yes, she does. Yoshiko did too. She was sometimes confused about where sentences end when she tried to read Japanese, but her comprehension was pretty good. Noriko is not a language scholar but bilingual. [Laughs] She speaks English and Japanese. [Laughs] She reads Japanese books and American books. She is helpful. She is my interpreter. [Laughs]
BB: Is Koichi pretty much Americanized?
MO: He seems that way.
BB: How about your grandchildren?
MO: Yes. They don't speak Japanese at all. They are taking Japanese classes at school and learning how to read and write. They sometimes send me a letter written in Japanese. I offer to write them back in Japanese. They say, "Grandma, I can read what you write, but understanding is another thing." It is funny because they can read and write, but they don't necessarily understand the contents. [Laughs] They are trying.
BB: Do they speak Japanese?
MO: Not really. They don't really speak it. They can say "hello" and "thank you." [Laughs] That's all.
BB: They can say "sushi," right?
MO: They can. [Laughs] They know "sushi" and "tempura."
