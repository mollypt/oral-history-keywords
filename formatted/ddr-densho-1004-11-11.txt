gky: After the war, you were -- you got out of the army. It was after the war, wasn't it?
GH: Yes. Well, when the war ended I was asked to accompany Colonel Burden and General Ho Ying-chen. He was the recipient of the surrender. The three of us flew to Nanking for the Japanese surrender. I was assigned to General McClure as his personal interpreter. And there was some experience which I'd like to share with you. But when I first arrived at the airport, there were a handful of GIs and a handful of nationalist Chinese. The rest were Japanese. And I was accompanied -- I went to the hotel with Colonel Burden. The colonel says, "Grant, let's go for a walk." Incidentally, I was a three-day second lieutenant. The colonel and I walked on the street and here comes the Japanese soldiers. They were just walking down the street, and the colonel said, "Grant, what are you going to do?" I said, "Let's get the hell off." I pushed him off the street and let them walk by. The Japanese are very cocky. As a matter of fact, even after the surrender, I talked to Lieutenant Colonel Okasawara, a staff officer. He said, "We didn't lose the war in China." He said, "We dropped, we're surrendering because of the Emperor's orders." They were very cocky.
At the day of surrender, I recall we were provided with a 1939 Chevrolet. There was five of us, and we couldn't get in. I sat on the colonel's knee to get to the hall. But at the surrender hall, I sat just behind General MacArthur and I had a very good view. A 1940 Roadmaster bike, Roadmaster pulls up and warrant officer got off. And then I saw another 1940 Roadmaster with a warrant officer, and I started giggling, recalling how I got to the surrender hall. But it was a very short but very solemn ceremony led by General Okamura, commander of the Chinese, I mean [Japanese] army in China. And when the ceremony was over, the general said, "Grant, we're going to Manchuria." I said, "General, all I have on is a khaki uniform. I'm not prepared to go to Manchuria." He said, "Take my plane, go back and get your clothes, and come back." When I returned to Chunking, Major Snyder greeted me. He says. "Grant, you're the third man on the list for rotation." I says, "Major, I have the general's plane waiting for me." He said, "I didn't hear you. I'm telling you, you're the third man on the list for rotation." I said, "How soon can I leave?" He said, "There's a plane leaving for Calcutta at six o'clock." I said, "Thank you." I got to the airport, you know which plane I got on. So I left San Francisco and I returned to New York via the Suez Canal. When I saw the Statue of Liberty, the tears came down, and I said, "If they had only told me I had a roundtrip ticket, it would have made things a lot easier." [Laughs] I was discharged at Camp McCoy November 8, 1945.
