AD: Your time at Minidoka, or let's start it this way, actually. I guess, what happened after Puyallup?
GA: Puyallup, we were there for four months, and after that, we were put on a train to head out to our final destination which was Minidoka, Idaho. At that time, we didn't know where it was, and so anyway, we wound up going down to Portland, up the Columbia into Pendleton, Baker, Ontario, into Boise and into Twin Falls and beyond into the desert. The, that would be the south, south Idaho, in the desert. And there, they loaded us on a truck and, or convoy, and we went off again for another few miles, way into the desert. And we could see as we were approaching, big clouds of dust. And I thought that maybe that's a desert storm. But as it turned out, as we approached the entrance to the internment camp, that was the bulldozer and the carpenters working to finish off the barracks to house the 9,000 immi-, not immigrants, internees to be held for the duration. And here again, as we entered, there were soldiers lined up with guns. And barbed wires all the way around the camp, and guard towers at strategic points to, to watch us. And everything was pointed inward, not outward, and they were telling us, "Well, we're protecting you," but sure didn't look like it.
AD: What did it feel like to be at camp? What do you remember feeling?
GA: Well, there were good times and bad times. The good times were that we used to have dances and socialize, and that would break the monotony or the, the depression, depressing thoughts of being in a camp. And as I stated before, the school started in 1943 for the grade school and the junior high and the senior high school. And I had, I was supposed to be a 1944 graduate, but I decided to graduate in one year, in '43, not knowing what, what was in store for me beyond the following year. And as it turned out, it was a good thing that I graduated that year, because the following year, I was caught in the draft and I would have probably not finished high school.
