TI: So let's talk about school. So you, we talked a little bit about school in camp, you said it wasn't the best, it wasn't that good.
LH: No.
TI: So tell me about the schools.
LH: Well, let me tell you about my walking to school. Because I'm walking alone and I don't know what it's going to be like, and I run into some girl, her name was Doris, actually. She just greeted me and said, "Do you want to walk together?" And it's like I'd lived there all my life. And I think I was sort of prepared, because there weren't any other Asians in that area, I was just sort of flabbergasted. And then we'd meet other kids, and they never looked at me differently, you know, like they didn't ask me what moon I'd come from or anything. [Laughs] So in that school, all the kids would walk before school started, they'd all walk two or three across, and they'd all walk to the end of the building, turn around and come back, so eventually say hi to everybody in the school.
TI: Oh, interesting. So it's kind of like those lines where...
LH: Like where the baseball players...
TI: Yeah, you do that, you'll walk by, they always do the high five.
LH: Uh-huh, and the whole school would do that, all the kids in the school, the first day, which is neat. And I was made to feel a part of that.
TI: Were the students in any way prepared for you?
LH: You know, I keep thinking they just treated me like I've been there all my life, and that seemed unusual because there weren't any other Asians there. I don't think in the whole history of that school there were, probably could count them. (Narr. note: As far as I know, in my graduating class, there were 7 black students, 1 Latino student, and 1 Asian American, me.)
TI: How about other ethnic minorities like blacks?
LH: There were, uh-huh.
TI: And they were treated the same?
LH: Well, you know, my perspective, in one class, it was a civics class, this one black young man, he was a pianist, the pianist, incredible. And if another black kid acted up, he would say, sort of excuse him kind of thing. But I didn't, I wasn't aware of discrimination.
TI: So talk about the education.
LH: Okay. Well, the kids were great, but my English class, that was a different experience for me. And I remember it because the kids would, the teacher would call on a child, and they'd stand up and they'd read the sentence and name every part of speech, including words that I'd never heard before. And I thought, oh, my goodness, this is Greek. And I about died, and I did go to her after class and explained where I'd come from. And she was very kind, and she'd only call on me for very obvious kinds of things.
TI: Like nouns, verbs? [Laughs]
LH: [Laughs] Pronouns.
TI: Pronouns.
LH: Actually, yeah, right. So I was grateful for that, but I was always like this in class, just crunched up as small as I could be. But they didn't make fun of me or anything, which was pretty neat.
TI: Well, and then eventually I guess you'd learned all that, too, by being around it?
LH: You know, I should have learned more than I did. Aki, my husband, he, in Moses Lake, received an excellent education on English, he knows all of that, and I'm still, it's not a strong thing for me.
TI: How about the other topics? Did you notice anything else?
LH: Civics was the same way because in camp we weren't aware of what's going on in the world. And these kids, they're juniors, right, and they would talk about current events. And a foreign subject again for me, but I managed to not have to participate too much.
TI: Did the topic ever come up about where you had just come from? Did they know about the camps?
LH: You know, I don't think so, and I'm puzzled by that. I think maybe there was something... you know, I'm just puzzled by it. Was there a newspaper article to say that some of us will be coming out of camps and treat us kindly or something? I don't know. But we were certainly treated well. And I had that one experience where, when I went to camp and I saw all the Japanese and I was in shock. So in this experience in Des Moines, we would go to the Drake Relays, and I would go with some of the classmates, and I was totally enjoying the relays and totally forgetting myself, totally forgot myself. Until I went to the bathroom, I looked in the mirror, and I was shocked. Because there, instead of a white person, there was an Asian looking back at me, and that was an interesting thing. That's my second culture shock.
TI: Because you had so much just been a part of that community.
LH: Yeah, I was just part of it, yeah. Isn't that great?
