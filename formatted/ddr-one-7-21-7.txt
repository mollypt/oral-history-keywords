BB: You have many interesting stories. So you came over the States in 1957. Could you tell us about your tofu store and also what was around in the neighborhood when you came over?
MO: [Looking at a map] Let's see. I remember there was a Japanese school around here. It was from two blocks from here. There was also a newspaper publisher, wasn't there?Oshu Nippowas the name?Oshu Nippo. Teikoku Shokai Company was here, Mr. Matsushima was running the business. He had a post office there and contracted a lot of work for the consulate. I remember Mr. Takami was there to do the contact work for the consulate. I didn't have a passport, and I remember he was helping to have one issued for me. He knew a lot of Japanese people. That was before the current Anzen company was there. Fathers and uncles. I also remember Mr. Somekawa, he had a seafood store. I think it was called Nichibei. And Mrs. Saito. She was a widow and had a son and two daughters and had a grocery store, Star Saito. I think it was on Third. Do you see? She was there. I also remember a restaurant called Kusunoya. Mr. Ogawa had a fishing store and lived around Burnside. Dr. Oyama was a dentist on the third. Dr. Oyama was a dentist there. Tsuboi Family was there too. I remember I bought a sewing machine from them when they went back to Japan. [Laughs] And...
BB: What was the atmosphere of the Japantown? You were around twenty-one, right? Were there a lot of young people?
MO: There were not a lot of young people there. When I came over from Japan, Mr./Mrs. Genmatsu who had a grocery store around Montgomery Ward encouraged me to come to work at the store. They had a lot of white customers coming to the store, and he/she told me that it would be a good place for me to learn English. He/she said it would be best to go to a night school to take English classes and speak English to white customers at the store. He/she encouraged me to come to work there to get real life experience to learn the language. So I started to work at the store as a sort of volunteer. [Laughs] I had flexible hours. The owner picked me up, and I sometimes left early, and sometimes stayed late. He/she was a teacher at my mother's Japanese school. We knew him/her very well, and he/she took a very good care of me. I went to work there to learn English. I spent a lot of time there. I didn't really hang around in the Japantown. Mr. Saito's daughter graduated from college and came back. She was a similar age, and I helped at the church with her if they had some activities going on. I was also close to Ms./Mr. Koyama, who was working for a newspaper publisher. I didn't really go to the Japantown for entertainment.
BB: Where did you live?
MO: We lived on the upper floor of the tofu store. [Laughs]
BB: How was it?
MO: It was a total junk house. [Laughs] It was just for sleeping. [Laughs] We knew a Buddhist priest Okihara from Seattle. He always stopped by at our house every time he came over from Seattle. I sometime woke up and found him praying in the house while I was sleeping. [Laughs] That always surprised me. He always stopped by. My father was a Buddhism fanatic back then. [Laughs] That's what I remember.
BB: Did a lot of people live on the upper floor of their store?
MO: Yes. The owner of Anzen, Teikoku Anzen, had a store on the lower floor and living space and rental rooms on the upper floor. Many people did that. Many people came back from a camp after the war, and they didn't have a place to live. Some people started a hotel to live in the building and generate income at the same time. Most stores had the owner sleeping or eating in the back room. When I came over, the store was at 322 North West Everett. My mother had a bedroom for herself in the tofu store building when I came over in 1955.
BB: What differences did you notice when you came back in 1955? Did you notice any?
[Telephone rings]
BB: What was different when you came back in 1955?
MO: Not much. My mother was still living in the tiny place, and I felt sorry for her. She had the store and was making tofu without even taking Sundays off. She was working from Monday through Sunday.
BB: Did many of the stores disappear around here? There are still some left.
MO: You mean in the downtown area?
BB: It must have changed a lot.
MO: It really changed. Teikoku is no longer around, and Mr. Oda and other people are gone. The Tsuboi family lived on Third when I came over in 1955. Kida family moved there too. I don't know where they moved from. The Kida family was at Teikoku. When I came over in 1955 after the war, the store was replaced by Anzen. It was on a corner but moved to a location between second and third. The move was right before or after 1955. I don't know if was in 1955, before that or after. I think they moved after the war. I don't know for sure. The original store was very small.
