TI: So you now go to Tule Lake, tell me what that's like. You're one of the first people there.
LH: Yeah. Well, we went on this rickety train with the curtains down, so the people couldn't see in or couldn't see out. And I know I sat right next to my mother, which I knew she was worried, just body language. So we got to Tule Lake, and it was a shock, because it's a desert, and we're used to green. So we got out, that I remember, that it's just a desert place. But the barracks... when my dad and my brothers, my dad especially, would write to George, he tried to be upbeat, which was, you know, you don't write to your kid saying, "This is an awful situation," and all that kind of stuff. So he described, we were in, my brothers had one room and my dad, excuse me, my two older brothers were in one room and we were in a separate room, and he describes it as being light and airy. It was a desert, I remember the dust, well, you know the stories that you've heard.
TI: But really dry and hot in the summer?
LH: It was very hot. We used to, again, right away they had activities for us, and we would go shell hunting, we would go digging in the sand with our strainers and dig for little bits of shell, which we then would come back, wash them, and take a craft class and learned how to make corsages and that kind of thing. So that was an activity.
[Interruption]
TI: So when you were at Tule Lake, you just talked about the craft activity, how about school? You were in sixth grade, what did they do for school?
LH: Oh, we were in barracks, they had schools, you know, they're all barracks. The instruction wasn't very good, instructors, because it was the beginning. In one letter my brother said he was offered a job to teach school, which he declined, because he knew he wasn't a teacher or prepared. So we had a lot of people like that, it was just high school graduates teaching.
TI: And do you remember who your teacher was?
LH: No, uh-uh. No, we didn't have good equipment, I remember that. And was it Tule Lake or was it Hunt? At Tule Lake we had very kind teachers. Her name was Mrs. Wells, and she didn't teach us very much in the way of... I knew we were supposed to be doing language and parts of speech -- in those days they used to teach parts of speech -- but she wouldn't go beyond. And we used to do diagramming, right, but she never did diagramming. But she was very kind, and she would read to us, but I don't remember instruction. So when I went to Des Moines, then I felt I was really handicapped because I didn't know what they were talking about in language.
TI: So this is after leaving Tule Lake, and at Minidoka, because you were at Minidoka, too.
LH: Yeah, right.
TI: So talk about why you transferred from Tule Lake to Minidoka.
LH: Oh, well, because Tule Lake was made into a reparation camp, right, to those who wanted to back to Japan. So my dad elected to go, not to do that.
TI: Do you remember, you were too young to, perhaps, fill out the questionnaire, but do you remember that process that people had to go through?
LH: No, uh-uh, I didn't even remember all the problems that were going on, the "no-no boy" and that kind of thing.
TI: But at some point you just knew that your family was going to move another camp. Do you remember any feelings towards people who left of stayed, in terms of differences?
LH: Uh-uh, I didn't.
TI: And how about your brothers? Because you had a, your oldest brother was serving already in the U.S. Army?
LH: Right.
TI: Did that ever cause any problems with anyone at the camp in terms of people questioning why?
LH: Uh-uh, no. He did come to visit once in uniform.
TI: Yeah, tell me about that, what was that like?
LH: Well, it was okay, we took pictures outside the gate. [Laughs]
TI: Because he wasn't able to come in?
LH: No, he could, yeah, he came in. I don't know. One thing we did at Tule Lake for recreation, we used to get permission to leave camp and climb that, we called it Castle Rock. And we would get lemons from the kitchen to sustain us, to quench our thirst.
TI: Oh, interesting. I've never... so for hiking, you would bring a lemon?
LH: Uh-huh, and it worked.
TI: I've never tried that.
LH: Okay, you could try that.
TI: I do a lot of hiking, but I've never thought of a lemon. Any other stories or memories about Tule Lake before we move to Hunt or Minidoka?
LH: Well, we were Camp Fire girls, they had those organizations. I met my very best friend there, and she became a life friend. So, you know, it had some positives.
TI: And where was she from before the war?
LH: She was from this area, Gig Harbor, this area.
TI: And what was it about her that made you guys such good friends?
LH: We were Camp Fire girls together. I don't know, we would walk to school together, and then she did not go to Hunt, but then we met again when she was at the university.
TI: Did you ever talk to her about what Tule Lake was like after you left?
LH: No, but if I were to talk about anything with anyone, I could have spoken with her. Because later we did talk a little bit about camp, we sort of have the same attitude. [Laughs]
