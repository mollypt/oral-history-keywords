BB: When you came to the States in 1955, did you feel any discrimination against you?
MO: I believe it was there, but I was embraced by white people around me. Everyone was very nice to me. I think there was a certain level of rejection against Japanese people, but I didn't experience any.
BB: How about your children?
MO: I don't think they went through any either. Right after we came over, Ko (Koichi) got into a fight with a white boy and threw the boy over. He was takingjudomartial arts classes in Japan. [Laughs] So he just threw the boy over. [Laughs] That surprised me. [Laughs] I asked what happened, and the teacher said it was not a big deal. [Laughs] He didn't have language skills to argue because it was just right after we came from Japan. I don't know which boy started the fight, I didn't ask. [Laughs] He had a fight. That's what I heard. [Laughs] That was probably the last one I know. He might have had a few more after that. The girls didn't have any problems. Everyone was nice to us.
BB: You have lived in the States for a long time. What do you like best about the country? I'm sure there are things you like and you don't like. What are they?
MO: Well, I don't know. I think it was the right decision to come over. I cannot come up with anything that I like or don't like right away. I am pretty old, and everyone has been so nice to me. I would imagine I would have been less active if I lived in Japan. I still drive and go wherever I would like to go. [Laughs] I do. I'm invited to join some groups and activities. I am over retirement age in Japan. I would have been an old lady and stuck at home. I am still joining with others, having chats with each other and enjoying an active life. I am too old to travel around, and that is sad. I cannot travel far. But I'm grateful.
BB: What is the most important Japanese cultural aspect you would like to pass on? Is it your family?
MO: What do you mean?
BB: I'm sorry. I wasn't clear. You might get more freedom in the States, but it is also important to preserve your culture. What do you think about that?
MO: Let's see. I still follow old customs. I don't think I can expect my daughters to do the same. For example, there are certain procedures I would like to follow for a funeral. I would like to thank the attendees for their support in a certain manner. I would like to deliver my gratitude. But I don't know my grandchildren's generation will pay much attention to those customs. There are rules and customs to follow in Japan among the family members and relatives, and it is a lot of work. It is a lot easier in this country without having to worry about them. Relatives sort of check each other in Japan to make sure we are following the tradition. It is very taxing. I still write to my old friends, only once a year, I have been sending a letter to them every year to this old age. I send Christmas cards out and receive letters back. I have received letters from a husband to let me know that the wife passed away sometime in the previous year. [Laughs] That was the end of that relationship. My friends in Japan are disappearing. I have fewer friends. I still keep in touch with my relatives like my cousins and their children, and they still contact me.
BB: I see. Here is the most important question for you. Do you like tofu?
MO: [Laughs] I don't eat it very often. I don't dislike it and should try to eat it more often. Enjoy tofu. [Laughs]
BB: I do eat a lot of tofu. [Laughs] Is there anything else that you would like to share with us?
MO: I don't think so.
BB: Thank you very much.
