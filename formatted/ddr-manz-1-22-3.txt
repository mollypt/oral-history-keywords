AL: Did you, were most of your friends Japanese American in high school, or...
YK: Yes, because our elementary school, well, we en masse went to Lincoln High School. And because we're from downtown Little Tokyo, most of the Japanese Americans went to Lincoln. Later, after Belmont High School was built, they, the closer one went to Belmont High School. Then a few went to Roosevelt High School in Boyle Heights. Well, there was, rivalry was pretty intense among the Japanese Americans. Belmont was filling... oh, their roster with Japanese Americans, Roosevelt High School has so many. So if we were after school, we would meet them on the streets and they were razzing each other, teasing each other if that team lost to the upper ones. So we had a good, good rapport with our community.
AL: Did you play sports in high school?
YK: I started to, yes. But somehow, I got sidetracked because I wanted to make money for the family. My older brother was deliveringRafu Shimpoon a bicycle, and the wholesale market district, which was around Seventh and Central, Ninth and San Pedro, and their residents around Tenth, Eleventh, Twelfth Street, on Central and San Pedro. There was a big population of Japanese Americans there. So I started tenth grade, Boyle Heights was my delivery site, and... well, one thing I was thankful for about delivering newspapers, I ran into, made more friends because I would see them occasionally at [inaudible] and playing sports, but it, it encouraged me to become more outgoing, because now they got to razz each other about the quality of their high school teams since we had two close by. And well, I think it made us more competitive.
AL: What was your favorite subject in high school?
YK: My favorite subject was, believe it or not, English. That's the subject I had more trouble with in elementary school. At elementary school, in the upper grades, we had English composition, but the emphasis was on diagram. Diagramming English simple sentences was so confusing to me. Still remember when it came to imperative sentences, the subject was "you," even though it was not in the actual sentence. And for the life of me, I could not understand why I had to substitute "you" in the diagram as the subject of the sentence when it was not written. [Laughs] That was the most confusing part of my English life.
AL: Did you have a favorite teacher in high school?
YK: Oh, yes. This was in chemistry, Ms. Quigley was a young teacher, probably just out of college, and she was, everyone had an infatuation for her. But she was very nice, and she made chemistry a very interesting subject for me.
AL: Did you have any Japanese American teachers?
YK: No, at that time, there were very few girls who went to college to further their experience. And no one thought of teaching at a school at that time. So we did not have, not in my acquaintance.
AL: What did, when you were in high school, what did you want to be when you, when you got older? Did you have a career goal?
YK: No, we did not. Most of us didn't have anything in mind, so getting ahead of the story, we saw our older brothers going to Trade Tech, which is a technical school just for those who had clever hands, fingers. So this was set up in the city for those who want to go into the radio.
AL: What -- oh, I'm sorry, go ahead.
YK: That was one of the trade school that became Frank Wiggins, that was a private school, taught radio techniques, and it was located right in downtown L.A., that's where my oldest brother went to learn radio broadcasting to become a technician, and become a shortwave technician, I guess. And to use his knowledge, went into Signal Corps when world war started, and he was drafted.
