JD: What else, if you think of people in the future, remembering what's most important about MIS, what would you like to, like them to know?
GH: Well, like, my personal opinion is that the MIS or the Military Intelligence, played a great role in the success of the Pacific War. Even General Willoughby, who was MacArthur's intelligence officer, said the MIS shortened the war by two years and saved countless number of GIs. I think Merrill even said that as for theNiseigroup, he said, "I couldn't have gotten along without them." There are many stories aside from the Merrill's Marauders where the MIS accomplishments have been considerable.
JD: In the beginning of the interview, you said you had two reasons you had to fight in the war. You had the enemies abroad and you also had the enemy of discrimination at home. By the end of the war, how did you feel about those two goals?
GH: Well, I said to myself, "I did my best," I took some satisfaction. I think, well, not myself, but in general, the performance of the 442, the MIS, I think did have considerable influence in the attitude. I think one of the turning point was Truman actually reviewed the 442 and I think that was a changing point. As a matter of fact, it was a very rainy day and his staff recommended that he call off the parade. But he says, "No, I know what these boys went through, I can stand a little rain." He reviewed theNiseis. I think they also had a, quite a welcome back parade in New York, also in Hawaii. I think these were some of the changing points. I like to think that we did perform well. Yeah.
JD: I don't have any more questions. Anything else?
GH: Well, I think there's one question about coming back? The last couple questions?
JD: Oh, what was it like returning to the U.S. after the war? Would you like to say more about that?
GH: Well, I just want to mention that it was such a sensation to see that Statue of Liberty. Here I was on a liberty ship and when we saw that, when the Statue of Liberty came in view, we all jumped. We hugged each other. It was a very satisfying experience. And I recall as the boat inched up to the shore, I could see the Stars and Stripes waving. When the boat docked, the band struck up and the song was "Sentimental Journey." I said to myself, "Journey, yeah. It was a journey I took on my own volition." I said to myself, "It was a trip around the world." In twenty-six months at government expense. And I said to myself, "I wish I had known I had a round trip ticket. It would have helped." But it gave me great satisfaction. It gave me a chance as a citizen to exercise my rights and duty.
But this is a great country, and country that acknowledges wrong. And under the Civil Liberties Act, Reagan did apologize to the people and made the token restitution. He also gave us permission to build a monument which is equal distance from the Union Station and the Capitol, a beautiful memorial. It's called the National Japanese American Memorial to Patriotism. It gives respect to, honors to the eight hundred and some odd KIAs, killed in action. It also has the names, a wall, named after the internment camps. It's a reminder that such an incident should not happen to anyone regardless of religion, race, or color. I recommend those who visit Washington D.C., visit the National Japanese American Memorial to Patriotism.
