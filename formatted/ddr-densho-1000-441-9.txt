TI: So you transferred from Tule Lake to Minidoka or Hunt. What were your impressions of Hunt?
LH: My impressions of Hunt?
TI: Yeah, was it different than Tule Lake, kind of the same, people seemed the same or there were differences?
LH: Oh, okay, we were back with Northwesterners. In Tule, most of the people were from California. In fact, we said the Portland people were going to go to Tule Lake, so whenever a caravan would come through, we'd look in the back of the caravan to see if there were people we knew, but they weren't they were Californians. They were people who'd been working out in the field, more tan than we were, so they never came.
TI: So you were always a little disappointed, because you were hoping to see them.
LH: Uh-huh. So in Hunt, then, we were back with Northwesterners, Portland people and then Seattle people.
TI: And what's the difference? So you're with Northwesterners, how is that different than being with Californians?
LH: Oh, well, the California people that we would see in the caravans, they were more tan than we were because they worked outside, right, on the farms and things? That's the only difference.
TI: How about language? Did they sound different than the Northwesterners?
LH: No.
TI: How about mannerisms?
LH: Not that I knew.
TI: Okay, so now you're back at Hunt. How about school? Was it about the same, or was that different?
LH: I, for me, the quality of education was not good in any of the camp schools. I remember in this, I think it was a civics class or something. We used to buy food and we'd put it in our desks and then we'd eat during class. [Laughs] That's what I remember. We didn't learn very much. I didn't learn very much, maybe other people did.
TI: Earlier you mentioned how when you were at the Portland Assembly Center, there were times where you'd say you wish you weren't Japanese so you wouldn't have to be in camp.
LH: Right.
TI: Did those feelings kind of continue, or did it change over time?
LH: No. I was, totally accepted the fact that, okay, I'm in camp, I'm Japanese.
TI: So at some point that kind of awareness...
LH: Right, uh-huh. It's just an acceptance of the reality.
TI: Stories about Minidoka, anything that comes to mind, any memories?
LH: Well, we did work on, everybody worked, you know, a desert. But I think what impressed me is the way people would transform the desert into gardens and things. And we had a garden between our two barracks, and I remember participating in irrigation, and we did raised beds. And my mother planted flowers next do the door, and I think people really tried, which was nice.
TI: Now, did your parents have jobs?
LH: My dad did. He worked in the newspaper office. My mother did not.
TI: What did your dad do in the newspaper office?
LH: Maybe he was a... I don't know. Since he was bilingual, maybe, I don't know, something like that.
TI: So maybe he was like a writer, one of the writers or reporters?
LH: You know, I don't know.
TI: And did he do this at Tule Lake or Minidoka?
LH: At Minidoka.
TI: Okay, I can probably go back and look at some of the old editions and see if I can find his name. So, yeah, that's interesting. Because yeah, it'd be theIrrigator.
LH: Right.
