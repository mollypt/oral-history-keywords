[This transcript is a translation of the original Japanese text.]
BB: What brought you back to the States in 1955?
MO: My mother was still here, and she had no intentions of going back to Japan. It was after the war, and I thought this country would be the better place for our children's future and education. We brought all three children with us. My husband had a job in Japan, and I could have found a job there too. We had a stable life, and it was not easy to give it up. I came over for the children's future. I don't know if that was the right decision or not. [Laughs] I wonder if it was.
BB: You spent one and half years back in the U.S. Was it in 1955?
MO: Oh, I spent one and half years here in 1937.
BB: Oh, I'm sorry. You were here for one and half years in 1937.
MO: I had already graduated and was ready to find a job. I came over. I couldn't find a good job because I didn't speak English. It was right before the second Sino-Japanese War, and the economy was not strong either. I ended up going back to Japan because I thought I would be better off there. I got married and had children, and brought the entire family back to the U.S. in 1955.
BB: How about your husband? Did you meet him through your family? You husband married into your family, didn't he?
MO: Yes.
BB: Could you tell us about theyoshicustom of having a husband marry into the wife's family?
MO:Yoshicustom. In Japan, families with daughters would have a man from another family marry into their family to carry on the family name. I am the only daughter, and my husband was one of our relatives. He came to our family to take over the family name. His family name was Isoshima, and he assumed my family name Ota.
BB: Was the marriage arranged?
MO: It was sort of arranged. [Laughs] My husband's father, my father-in-law, and my uncle made the arrangement. That's how it worked in Japan. [Laughs]
BB: Did you get married right after you went back to Japan?
MO: Yes, I did.
BB: You went back to Japan for your education in the first place. Did you work there?
MO: Yes. I wasn't working when I got married. My husband got drafted and was deployed to China after we got married. I didn't know what would happen to him. I didn't know if he could come back alive. My uncle offered to take care of our children and encouraged me to find a job. The school I graduated from offered me a position. That's where I worked.
BB: Were you a teacher there?
MO: Yes, I was.
BB: What did you teach?
MO: I was a home-ec teacher and teaching sewing and craft. I was teaching home-ec.
BB: Did you like your job?
MO: I did. I was trained for that.
BB: Did you continue to work during the war?
MO: Yes, I was working for a while during the war. My husband came back from the war and got a job in Nagoya city. I quit my job to go back to Nagoya, and we went back to Nagoya. That is when I stopped working. Nagoya city was air raided after that. The city was burned down. We went back to Okayama prefecture. I got an offer again to teach. Someone just asked me. [Laughs] I taught again in 1951, '52 and '53. I pretty much made up my mind to come back to the States at that point, and I told them that I could not commit myself for a long-term position. I was working to help at the same school.
