(JA: I want to kind of go back before the war a little bit and have you tell me -- actually first so we have it on tape, tell me your name and where you live now today.
AM: Okay, my name is Archie Miyatake. I live in Montebello, California. I am -- I just turned seventy-eight. When I was in camp I was about sixteen or seventeen, I believe, and I graduated there in the class of '44.
JA: Okay. Go back before the war and tell me a little bit about who your parents were, where they came from, and what they did here in the United States.
AM: Okay. My father is from Japan. He's -- he came here in 19-, gee, I can't remember) exactly what year it was, but his grand-, his father, or my grandfather, was here in southern California in 1906 after the earthquake in San Francisco, and then he called his three sons from Japan. He had a confectionary store in what is now the Little Tokyo. And upon his retirement he went back to Japan and he was, he bought some property and he was renting them out for income. Let's see, I forgot what year it was. Anyway, in 1933 my father decided to go back to Japan to take care of his father, who was gravely ill by that time. So he closed his studio and sold it to somebody and the whole family went back to Japan. He was there for one year. Meantime, those days we had to go on steamships, so when we left Hawaii, my grandfather passed away, so we never got there in time to take care of him. So he stayed there -- my father stayed there one year and came back, and the rest of the family stayed two more years. After we came back, my father started his business again in Little Tokyo.
So, he's had the studio all the way until the outbreak of the war, and then we were interned in Manzanar in 1942, May. So, he... we lived in Block 20, and one of the thing my father did without telling the rest of the family was -- he had snuck in a lens and some film holders. The reason for that is because all Japanese or Japanese American were forbidden to have camera, shortwave, or any arms. But he was determined to record the camp life. He thought of that even before he, going into camp, so he snuck in a lens and some film holder. And then after a few months in camp, my father called me into the apartment and told me what he had done. And so, and at that time he said that, "As a father, I have a responsibility." So I didn't know what he was talking about, so I said, "What are you going to do?" He says, "Well, we're in here and I have to take photographs of camp life," so that he could record whatever happened in camp, and also, "so that this kind of thing will never happen again." So he eventually found a carpenter who made a box to his specification, and he started taking photographs. And the early photographs that he took had no people in it. The reason for that is because he feared that if somebody ever saw him taking a photograph, then he might get reported to the internal police, so he took the photograph early in the morning or during the time everybody's in the kitchen, mess hall, eating, and he would take pictures during that time and just go without eating.
