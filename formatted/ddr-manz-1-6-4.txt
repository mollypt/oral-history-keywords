JA: What do you remember about the order to go to camp and how that affected your family?
AM: Well, it was kind of shocking because, you know, as a child growing up, we were always taught that Japan and United States were very good friends and this and that, and then this, on December 7th... every Sunday my father used to take me into Little Tokyo because we lived in Boyle Heights, East Los Angeles, and he would take me into Little Tokyo to go to Sunday, attend Sunday school. As we were going over this one bridge which goes over the Los Angeles River, there was a radio broadcast saying that Pearl Harbor was bombed by the Japanese. My father was shocked to hear that and I was really shocked. I thought that must be some sort of a mistake, but you know, I would hear that the rest of the afternoon. And then that night, the newspaper headline, you know, "Pearl Harbor is bombed by Japan," and the thing like that, and so I thought, well, I guess it really must have happened. So, well, I was -- one of the things I was afraid of was that my father being a photographer, I wonder if they're going to, the FBI is going to suspect him to be a spy or something because of his type of work. But, fortunately, there was nothing like that happened. In fact, little after that, the alien registration was started and my father got a special permission to go beyond 5-mile limit. Everybody had a limit from where you lived that you couldn't go any further than 5 miles. Well, being that the case, a lot of the people who had to have photographs taken for the alien registration could not go to the place where they would like to have a picture taken, so my father got a special permission to go to Oxnard and Camarillo, San Luis Obispo, to take these ID photographs for the people. And what he did was he had a panel truck and he made it all light-tight and made a darkroom in there, and wherever he was he would develop the film and print the picture and deliver it right then and there so that they could have it right away. I remember going on some of those trips with him.
JA: It sounds like he was one of the first 60-second -- 60-minute photo places.
AM: Yeah. [Laughs]
JA: He was a very clever man in many ways.
AM: Yeah, so, in that way, you know, his thinking was pretty advanced compared to some of the other photographers.
