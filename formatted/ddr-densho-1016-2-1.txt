SE: My father-in-law, they lived with us. This is the only Japanese-speaking church there, Mother, my father-in-law and mother belonged to the church and they came in Sunday. And when they... see, at that time, it was the Grant Street church, and then they bought this property and started the building. So my father-in-law was a stone mason in Japan, and he built that stone wall in the front. And my husband brought his tractor down to help move some of the big rocks. But... and the man that was kind of instrumental was Mr. Ishikawa, who was a gardener, and he had a lot of things to do with fixing the beautiful garden and planning on how he wanted the walls to be built. So it was... I'm trying to remember the year. But my mother and father-in-law were supposed to go to Japan, and everything was all set and they had their tickets and everything, and so Dad had to hurry up and get that wall up before they left for Japan because the winter was setting in, they had to have it all fixed up. So there was kind of a rush that year.
AD: Seiko, even though maybe there was some animosity there, did the church provide community for Japanese Americans?
SE: Yeah, they did. This was, the church was the main place for the people to congregate and come. And like I said, the Reverend and Mrs. Cobb opened their home and the church to all the people and we felt very welcome here. She was like a mother to all the young people that came because their folks were already in camp. And so it was a haven for them, yes. So I don't know how much they -- it was very interesting, I have to tell you about this thing. Reverend and Mrs. Cobb were southerners. I can't remember if they were from Georgia or where. But they were missionaries in Japan, and they knew how to speak Japanese. In fact, they read the Japanese scriptures and all. But it was very interesting to hear someone speak Japanese with a southern accent. [Laughs] It was so different. But they were an amazing couple.
