AL: When did you, when did you decide to go on the agriculture furlough? How did you find out about that?
YK: We had a softball season the first, first year, first summer. And you can play only so many games even though other blocks had teams, too, but our league ended, oh, in the fall. So that's when people went back to camouflage nets. When the fall season came, we had recruiters from agricultural department, they were [inaudible] workers to harvest or whatever. So they sent message to camp, potato picker, sugar beets, wheat harvest, fruits, whatever. And so came to the camp office, the camp office put the sign out, and everyone heard about it, so people started signing up. And they were to be in charge of the regional office in the farm community. So we had a choice of where to go, Idaho, Utah, Midwest. Oh, there were many, many opportunities. The next year it got better, we had better things to do agricultural-wise.
AL: Where did you go the first furlough?
YK: We went to Idaho Falls.
[Interruption]
AL: So we were talking about Idaho Falls. What did you do there?
YK: Well, we went to work for a Mormon family.
AL: Mormon?
YK: Mormon, yes. And his crop wasn't the best, so when we topped the sugar beets, they were small in weight. And sugar beets have to be at least four, five inches in diameter, and we didn't get those sizes at all, and it took us longer to fill a truck. But we survived that. Next crop was his brother's farm next door, and his brother's crop was much bigger, heavier. By that time, though, it was getting a little frosty, mid, almost end of November and early December. So it was harder work because the farmer would plow up the rows, but the ground was getting frosty, and even though the beets were kind of out in the open, that mud, mud would make them heavier. And in order to top their beets, we had to maneuver the beets so we could cut the greens close to the meat as possible. Well, we had lots of muscle soreness, but we made out money-wise because it was, took less time to fill the truck.
AL: How much were you paid?
YK: Oh gosh, I don't really remember.
AL: Better than camp?
YK: Oh, yes. Oh, yes, better than.
AL: Where did you live?
YK: Well, we had a, most farms had house for the workers, even though it was only with a wooden stove. And we had to chop firewood to do the cooking, or we had three wives, three wives were along with us, so they were able to do the cooking. And we had one little young girl, three, three and a half, who just learned to sing "white Christmas," and she would entertain us nightly, singing that same song, and we had a good time with her.
AL: Were these other couples out of camp?
YK: Oh, yes, same, on the same softball team.
AL: What softball team was that?
YK: We were called, we called ourselves the Has-Beens.
AL: And was Masa on a team, also?
YK: No, no, it was all men.
AL: Just guys, uh-huh. The, when you were topping beets, did the women work with the men?
YK: No.
AL: What did they do during the day?
YK: Well, I don't know. [Laughs] They had no radio, no television. Well, maybe they went to pick eggs.
