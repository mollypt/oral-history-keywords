AD: What did you think about during that time in terms of civil liberties and your freedom? What did you think about when you were in jail?
GA: Well, I don't know how to take this, or how to take your question there because my liberty was taken away, but then I was put in prison and I'm still, I never got my liberty. Only things they said was, "You're classified 1-A, you're an American citizen, you go to the service." Well, I didn't like that very well. So...
AD: Why?
GA: Well, here again, I'm gonna ask you a question: wouldn't you feel bad if you were asked that question? 'Cause, 'cause you were just being kicked around for the past two years. And just because the government said, "Well, you're fit for the service, you go or else," I think that was not very fair, so I felt bad about it. In fact, as the, the boat took us to our residence for the following two years, well, my thoughts went back to the time two years prior, when we were all living in Seattle. And because we looked like an "enemy alien," being Japanese, they incarcerated us, put us in assembly center, sent us to the internment camp and guard us, guarded us, and all of a sudden they decided, "Well, you're fit to go to the service, and if you don't go, well, we'll put you away." I'm just thinking, "Boy, that's a heck of a note," and here it's been roughly two and a half years since we were pulled out, and here we're back fifty miles from where we started two years. And were are we going? To a penitentiary. So, I mean, this was one of the low, low part of my life. I felt very bad about it. And to have the, their own community turn their backs on us, I felt bad, yeah.
AD: Tell us more about that. How did, how did your community respond to your resistance?
GA: Well, a lot of it was a write-up in the paper, and that is the camp paper, the outside paper, and that is newspaper. And they wrote us up as 'no-no boys,' they're draft-, they didn't call us draft resister, they called us draft dodgers, "They're scared to go to the army, and so they're no good." And the community was brainwashed by that kind of a thing 'cause they read, the believed what they read. And what they were trying to do, especially the, each center, were trying -- Minidoka was known to be a very, very obedient group, that they did everything the government told them. And so they had figured that everybody should go into the service when asked, and when they found out that I was refused to go, why, they started to read about our story and they just turned our back on us. And that's one of the things that happened while my dad was incarcerated elsewhere. My mother had gone through a lot of isolation, people didn't talk to her, and she kind of lived a life all by herself, an isolated life.
AD: What, what was your perspective at the time about those who did choose to serve?
GA: I figured these are people, they decided to go because they thought they should. So I had no opinion as to what's, whether they're wrong or not. I thought they did what they wanted to do. And so, and I respected them, and I expected them to respect me for what I did. But as it turned out, when you have a one against the crowd, you're nobody.
AD: Did you feel, in terms of, in terms of patriotism, did you feel like you were also being patriotic as much as those who served in the 442nd? Maybe talk about patriotism and what it means to be patriotic, and how you were patriotic in a different way.
GA: Well, that's the way I looked at it, because I'm trying to find, fight my own battle, but it's in my own country. And what was that I'm fighting for? My liberty, pursuit of happiness, all that. The difference, I think, between the vets and me is the vets had the idea, "I'm going to go out there and prove that I'm a good citizen." Whereas I said, "I'm a good citizen, but give me my citizens before I go into the service," my thoughts are, "It's not going to do me any good if I go out there and serve and get killed, no use getting your citizenship then. I want it now." But the government didn't see it that way.
AD: Did you think of yourself, you were young, did you think of yourself as courageous, as an, you were nineteen.
GA: No, I just thought that I'm just doing what I thought, I felt comfortable with, not knowing what I'm going to walk into. But yeah, I felt, or I did what I felt I should.
AD: Yeah, that's important, that's really important. Tell us about what changed. When you, what happened when you got of, out of jail? How did that, how did that come about?
GA: Well, after serving my time, why, they suited us up with a new suit, and complete suit, and twenty-five dollars in our pocket to get a bus to head on home. By then, by that time, my parents, the camps had all closed, and my parents had made their way to, back to Seattle. And so prior to leaving the institution, we had visitors, and we got words that the veterans were very much against us, and that we're gonna be confronted by them. But as it turned out, it never did happen, so I'm so happy that it didn't, 'cause otherwise, we may have said some bad words and maybe gotten in fights. And so that's one thing that I'm happy we never had happen, and it's amazing what time does. It just heals all the wounds. I happened to go around with a commander's wife, she was divorced -- I mean, not divorced -- a widow, and she and my wife was good friend. And when my wife died, well, I thought, "Well, I guess I'll take her out and see what she says." And she didn't argue with me, she thought it was okay. She wanted to know why I did it, and I gave her the explanation. And so once I started that, I thought, "Well, I might as well go down to the Vets Hall. They're going to kick me out, so be it, I'll find out." So I went there and all of 'em, they greeted me with open arms. And their thoughts were, "Well, shoot, you did what you thought was right, so I've got nothing against you." I have a couple of friends that their brothers had got killed, and they all didn't hold anything against me. So I found out that the majority of the fellows, when you meet up with them, they were, they were real nice, which was quite a relief.
