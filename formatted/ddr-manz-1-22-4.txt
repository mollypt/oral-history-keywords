AL: What year did you graduate from high school?
YK: Yes, it was a four-year college, four-year school, so 1933. So it was 1928 to 1933, summer of 1933.
AL: And what did you do right after high school?
YK: That's what I was saying, we had no goals, most of us had no goals in mind. And we saw our older friends going to Los Angeles Community College -- not a community -- Junior College. It was called junior college, and so many of us went, well, all of us went there. The few who had means went there to go to Berkeley. One or two who had exceptional brainpower went there for a year or two and transferred to Caltech. Some of my friends transferred to UCLA or Berkeley because they had the means. And our family, being so large, were, we had to kind of earn our way to further our education, so we went to L.A. Junior College. Well, we had really no majors to further our education, but I went into journalism. But journalism was a dead end because we had newspapers in town, we had a Hearst afternoon paper, or the [inaudible], which was an afternoon paper. We had theLos Angeles Times,and we had another one calledDaily News. Well, after graduation from City College, Los Angeles Junior College, we applied for, at each newspapers, and we were not welcomed because at that time, they were not hiring minorities. So we ended up in the retail markets, fruits and vegetables, the flower, florists, wholesale and retail, and the wholesale produce markets. There were plenty of jobs for those who wanted to work in the fruit stand, we called them fruit stand because they had fruits and vegetables separate from the regular markets. They had stands in front of the market on rollaway drawers where we served customers. And the city of L.A. had an ordinance prohibiting customers from picking, picking fruits and vegetables to buy. There was an ordinance that the city tried to enforce, but luckily for us, we had that ordinance, so we were able to serve persons when they wanted to buy fruits and vegetables. And that opened the door for the ladies. Ladies were, well, nicer, more pleasing to talk to, so the customers would prefer the ladies to serve them, pick what they wanted to buy. And so for the ladies, there were plenty of opportunities to become sales, salesperson in the fruits market.
AL: When you applied for the newspaper job, what did they say when they turned you down?
YK: Well, of course, there were no openings at that time.
AL: That's all they would say, is "no openings"?
YK: Yes. And the reception that some persons would project to us was, "What are you doing here looking for a job?" something like that. And well, the feeling was not very pleasant. Well, I don't know, it's... because at that time, there were very few Japanese Americans working for Caucasian owner enterprises. Like the markets, well, the markets were... they had subleased the fruits and vegetable markets, department, to Japanese owners, so they were working for Japanese owners instead of the American owners. So very, very few Caucasian-owned concerns outside of the, probably the wholesale markets. They probably owned, owned a house that the wholesale market was a house. They probably leased out so many square feet of office space or display area for other vegetables, too.

In the markets, again, depending on where you worked, there, market employees, I mean, the retail markets, were owned by several different Japanese owners. And many of them had chains, several wholesale, I mean, produce, several departments, fruit and vegetable departments in different stores.
