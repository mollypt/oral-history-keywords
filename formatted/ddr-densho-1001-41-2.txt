JD: What led you to volunteer for   the army, before Pearl Harbor?
GH: I was drafted and I was inducted   into the Army three days before Pearl Harbor. But I enlisted in the   Army Air Corps. And the reason for my enlistment was that it did   give me a choice of branch of service. It was my desire to become   an aircraft mechanic.
JD: Was there a tradition in your   family of going into the military? Career in the military?
GH: Not really because during the   war my brother, who attended Kyoto Imperial University, did serve   as a language instructor at Boulder, Colorado. He subsequently   worked with the State Department. He retired as a foreign service   officer. I have a younger brother, Bill, who was the manager of an   estate and he was exempt from draft. The next brother, he served in   a chemical weapons unit in CONUS. He served in the U.S. And I have   another brother who served with the engineering corps and served in   Japan during the occupation. I have, the youngest, he went to Yale   to learn the Chinese language and he served during the Korean   War.
JD: Did you have close relatives,   cousins or uncles, who were in the Japanese military?
GH: Well, I did have a distant   relative who was a lieutenant general in the Japanese army. I had   cousins who were in the army, yes, in Japan.
JD: After Pearl Harbor was bombed,   how did the U.S. government respond to the Japanese Americans who   were already in the military? Did they take any actions?
GH: Yes they did. I think these were   primarily affected those who were serving on the West Coast.   Because these people were reclassified as 4-C, which meant "enemy   alien," and they were discharged at the convenience of the   government. In my case, being in the Army Air Corps, I was   transferred to Jefferson Barracks, Missouri. When I arrived there,   they put me in what they called "protective custody." And I   happened to join twenty some other Japanese Americans who were   already stationed there. The reason for the protective custody, I   understand, was that during physical examination, Japanese   Americans... the environment was such that they had to take them   out and complete the physical examination in a separate room. We   were under protective custody for forty days and then we were   discharged and we were assigned to various flights. During that   period I served as a flight clerk and training clerk. I think it   was June or the middle of 1943, we were all discharged from the air   corps and reassigned to other units.
