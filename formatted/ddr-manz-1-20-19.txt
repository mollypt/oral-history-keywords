AL: And   then you came back to Los Angeles in, what year did you say, 1946,   maybe?
TO:   Thereabouts, yeah. 'seven, maybe, time in Elko.
AL: ...and   back to, you were talking about East L.A., and several families   living together?
TO: Yeah,   there were my father's cousin and their family, and this man who we   called our uncle, and his family, so there were three families in,   under one roof.
AL: And   was this a rented house, or did...
TO: Rented   house.
AL: Were   there other kids in the family?
TO: Yeah,   there was probably all together six, six kids.
AL: Six   kids, and how many adults?
TO: Six   adults.
AL: And   how long did you all live together?
TO: Oh, I   would say, probably six years or so, so that my cousin's family   could get on their feet, get their own place.
AL:   About... when you were coming back at that time, do you have   recollections of emotions about coming back to California, or   things you heard or saw, your reception coming back to California,   were you scared to come back, were you excited to come   back...?
TO: Well,   I don't know whether we had any Japanese American students at the   elementary school that I attended. It's in East L.A., Lorena Street   School. Could be that the Japanese Americans returned to other   areas, maybe closer to Boyle Heights or... and, but it seemed to me   that my brother and I were among the first Japanese Americans   there, and so, I had fights all the time. People called names,   "dirty Jap," "Ching-ching Chinaman," and I would fight them. We had   a tunnel from the school, under the street, on Lorena, and I said,   "I'll meet you in the tunnel." And that's actually a kind of a good   thing, because in East L.A., it serves you well. If people know   you're willing to fight, they leave you alone. [Laughs] And that,   while it was probably considered, in junior high, as a dangerous   area, with gang fights and whatever else and all, I never was   frightened. No one ever, ever bothered me.
AL: Did   you have friends? I mean, were there kids that you considered close   friends at that time?
TO: Oh,   yes. And on my block, you know, whether they were Briana, who was   Jewish, or there were Mexican Americans, just, in school, at   Stevenson Junior High School, we had Armenians students, Malacan,   Russian, American Indian, just everything. And we all probably were   poor, but we didn't know it. [Laughs]
AL: So the   kids who were taunting you and calling you "dirty Jap," and those   kind of things, were those mostly Caucasian kids, or were they   minorities?
TO: Yeah,   but it didn't last long. It didn't last long.
AL: And   was that because you were beating them up in the tunnel, or was it   because other kids defending them, or teachers intervening?
TO:   Probably a combination of all that. But I remember elementary   school as being a very happy time. I was poor, so I remember I had,   I was very tiny and skinny, and in order to be on the lunch   program, you had to work in the cafeteria and these ladies, they   would try to give me the easy jobs. You had to scrape the dishes,   and things like that, and...
AL: So   this was like a relief program? For working in the cafeteria, you   got free lunch?
TO: Yeah,   so you'd get a meal. But I remember the teachers were all very   wonderful in elementary school.
