JD: I'm interested... you knew that   the West Coast Japanese Americans had all been put in camps by a   certain time, by '42. I'm interested in, what did you think about   the government's response to you and other Japanese Americans who   were already in the military? You had already told them that you   were willing to serve. Did that reaction seem strange to   you?
GH: Well, we're citizens. I could   see having them place my parents who were "enemy"... who were   classified as "enemy aliens." But being an American citizen, I   didn't think that would affect us. But there was no differentiation   between the citizen and non-citizen and they were evacuated. There   were 120,000 evacuated into ten separate camps. I did have a first   time experience, after I graduated from the Military Intelligence   School -- which was in Camp Savage -- I did apply for furlough.   When I arrived at Tule Lake, California, it was in the desert...   and I was taken aback to see rows and rows of tarpapered barracks   behind barbed wire. When I looked up I saw sentries wearing the   same uniform as mine with a machine gun facing inward. That really   hit me hard. I then entered the camp. There was a soldier who   escorted me to the barracks where my parents were living. When I   met my parents and my siblings, we greeted each other with smiles,   but inside we were crying. It was very difficult. It was very   devastating to find my parents and siblings deprived of their   comfortable home, deprived of their rights, deprived of their   dignity and placed behind barbed wire. As far as my siblings were   concerned, their only crime was that they looked like the enemy. My   stay at the camp was short. When I left the camp and boarded the   train to return to my camp, I had mixed feelings. I was angry. I   was confused. Things went through my mind... I said, as a child I   did, each morning we stood and pledged allegiance to the flag. With   the allegiance saying that "liberty and justice for all." It meant   nothing. It really hurt to see my siblings behind barbed wire. I   think it was at that time I realized that we were fighting two   wars. One against the enemy from abroad and discrimination at home.   I did have a very strong feeling that I have to... I have a   responsibility. My mission was to do my best, hoping that it might   help relieve my parents from behind barbed wire.
JD: Why were they at Tule Lake and   not, say, Minidoka?
GH: I don't know.
JD: How were you treated by the   non... the other soldiers in your unit, non-Japanese Americans at   first, and then did that change?
GH: When you say unit, are you   talking about the Merrill's Marauders?
JD: Well, at first, after Pearl   Harbor, I'm sure the feelings probably changed. I'm wondering how   were you treated by the other people at that point?
GH: Well, when I was transferred   to... I was transferred shortly after the war broke out, I was   transferred to Jefferson Barracks, Missouri. Of course I had that   experience of forty days in confinement. But after I was released   from the protective custody, I didn't feel any discrimination. As a   matter of fact, I did serve as a flight clerk and [inaudible]   training clerk. I did, I was promoted to corporal. So, in a station   where they had recruits, being a non-commissioned officer was a   privilege.
JD: You know, something, when I   thought about it, there was something that struck me as very   strange. The government said that 120,000 Japanese Americans were   under suspicion, that any one of them might be a spy or a saboteur.   And yet the Japanese Americans who were already in the military, it   seems as if they should have been the first people who were   suspected, and you didn't see any of that.
GH: Well, actually, as they... there   was a finding. They did have an investigation afterwards. They did   find that the evacuation was based on lack of leadership --   political leadership -- wartime hysteria, and discrimination. And I   would add there was also economics involved. There was a lot of   people wanted the Japanese to leave. They would say that the   Japanese had the most rich farms. But the reason for that is   because they built it up to be a rich farm.
JD: How did you feel about the U.S.   going to war against Japan when you had family and relatives   there?
GH: Well, it really hit me hard.   Because to feel that my parents' country did strike U.S... that was   very difficult to handle, yes.
JD: Did you hear news about   grandparents or other relatives in Japan... how the war was   starting to affect them?
GH: No. There was no communication   whatsoever.
JD: So, until after the war, you   knew nothing really about what was happening?
GH: Right.
JD: Can you say a little more about   how, knowing how your family were all in camp behind barbed wires,   how that affected your own sense of loyalty to the U.S. and   patriotism?
GH: As a matter of fact, maybe I   felt strongly that I had to prove something, to prove that I was a   true loyal American. That led me to volunteer for the Merrill's   Marauders.
JD: It seems from the record of the   Merrill's Marauders, the MIS, and the 442, as if maybe every single   Japanese American in the U.S. military felt the same way you did.   Do you think that's true?
GH: I think most did feel that way,   yes. We didn't communicate, say we're gonna do this or that, but   inside that's how I felt.
JD: Did this come... do you think   this came from the way you were raised? Values from, cultural   values or family...
GH: I think it has much to do, yes.   Although my father... I've heard of others saying that their   parents had told them to take care of themselves but to perform to   the best of their ability and die if you must, but don't bring   shame to your family. I think that was a common strain of thought   among us.
JD: I've heard Senator Inouye say   that that's what his told him.
GH: Yes, uh-huh. All I recall is my   father saw me off at camp, railroad station. He said, his words   were, "These are very difficult times." He said, "I want you to   take care and I want you to do your best."
JD: What did you think would happen   to your farm and your home and the community back in Kent?
GH: Well, I was already in the army   and I wasn't concerned about the farm. I was primarily concerned   about my family being "enemy aliens."
JD: In some places there were   neighbors, non-Japanese neighbors and friends who would take care   of farms. Was that true in Kent?
GH: I think there were some   things... there was a family across the street from us, a dairy   farm, the Schulers, and I think they did accommodate my... some   goods. I don't know whether it was furniture or albums or what have   you, but I think they did assist us.
JD: What was the situation in Kent   with the farm when you came back after the war?
GH: None of us came back to Kent   after the war. My parents went to Minneapolis. They resettled in   Minneapolis. I have seen the farm and the house is in very   ill-repair.
JD: Did they go back to farming in   the Minnesota area?
GH: No. My father worked in a hotel   and my mother worked as a seamstress.
