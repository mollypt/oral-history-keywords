JD: Can you tell us a little bit about specific interrogations or events in Burma related to your work?
GH: Yes. Well, I interrogated many POWs. But there's one that really stands out, and that is a POW, he was a second lieutenant and he was brought in by a stretcher. It was late in the evening. I looked and he was stabbed by a bayonet in three positions, arm, hip, and leg. I heard that he was captured by the Gurkha. Gurkhas are very well-known Indian fighters. When he attempted to escape he was stabbed three times. He was brought in by a stretcher and he was in bad shape. So I said to myself, he needs medical attention. So I told the guards to take him and give him the proper medical attention and then bring him back early in the morning for interrogation. The following morning he was brought in and I said, "How was the medical treatment?" And he looked me in the eye and said, "You're a traitor." I said, "Traitor?" I said, "As you see, I'm an American soldier." I said, "You're fighting for your country. I'm fighting for mine. Don't you call me a traitor." I said, "For the color of our veins, the stream doesn't flow. But I'm fighting for my country and you're fighting for your country. I don't want to be called a traitor." Well, while I tried to interrogate him he refused to respond. He again used the word traitor. That really hit me hard. So I had the guard place him in the enlisted men's camp. That's the only camp we had. Don't put him the center of the camp. About a half an hour later I did approach him. He pulled my trousers. He said, "Mr. Interpreter, I want to die." I said, "How do you want to die?" He said, "I want you to shoot me." I said, "Shoot you?" I said, "I don't have any bullets to waste on you." But I said, "We do have a captured sword which you could demonstrate to the men how to commithara kiri." And with that I left. Shortly after I went back, he pulled my trousers again. He said, "Mr. Interpreter, I have a change of heart. Please get me out of here." So I said... I had him removed and he answered every question. The following day I saw Captain Chang who was also a graduate of MIS and he did make a comment. He says, "The old man liked the report." So I did get some feedback.

GH: Well, when you talk about the occupation, I was a civilian when I went to Japan. I was with staff legal section. I was with the Class B. It was divided into A, B, and C. A was the war crimes trials held in Tokyo. B and C were held in Yokohama and it was under the jurisdiction of the 8th army, General Eikenberger. I served as a DAC, Department of the Army Civilian. JD: Interesting... how, can you tell us a little bit about how Japan had changed between the time when you were a student there and when you went back there as a DAC connected with the legal affairs?
GH: Well, things had changed... times had changed. It was very difficult to say because I was there from 1947 to 1951. I do know that there was many reforms under General MacArthur. For instance the Land Reform, I think Women's Right to Vote. There's many constitution things. But, it did take a while for all that to be implemented. So it was a transitional period and I do recall one GI asked a lady, "How do you like democracy?" In Japanese she said, "Democracydou desu ka." How is the democracy? In Japan, in Japanese, it could be also mean it is more favorable... it's easier living. Demo-crassy. It's hard to put it in English, but it means that it is more comfortable under democracy. A lot of troops didn't know how to act. Because especially the first generation, they'd been under... they'd been brainwashed for so long that it did take time for them to adjust. I think that... I say that Japan lost the war but they won the peace. I think it's much more peaceful and I think the people are much better off.
