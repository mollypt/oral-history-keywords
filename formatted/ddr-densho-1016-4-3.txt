AD: What kind of, what did you lose physically, what did your family lose right after Pearl Harbor and after, what happened to your family?
GA: December 8th was the time when my dad -- that was Monday -- my dad went down to work. He had a shoe repair shop, oh, about three blocks from the immigration office. And my mother got a phone call saying that, "Come down and close up the store because this is the FBI. We're taking your husband over to the immigration office to talk to him for a few days, or maybe he should be out in a couple of weeks." So that was number one, that came about. And we brought, went down to see my dad. And the immigration office is very similar to a jail, 'cause it's all barred, and the hallways are barred. But anyway, he came out and spoke to us for a few minutes. And there was a soldier telling us to, that this is enough, so we parted. And that was our first visit to the immigration office. But in a couple of weeks, Dad called up and he says, "Would you send, bring down some toiletries and personal belongings and things, because they're sending a group of us to Missoula, Montana." And sadly, we went down with the, all his personal belonging, and we bade him goodbye. And that was the last time we saw him until two years later.
AD: What happened, then, to your mom and you and your siblings? Where did you end up?
GA: Oh, let me see. That was in March when they told us, all the Japanese Americans, to dispose of all their property, anything, their belongings, their home, whatever. And all you're gonna have is just a suitcase of personal clothing and things, and a shopping bag. Each person is allowed that much. The rest of it, dispose of it. So hurriedly, we tried the best we can, we could, and so many of the people, the people who were shopping for buys, they came and bought a lot of things like on, ten cents on the dollar. And rather than to lose a lot of their precious family heirlooms, they decided that they'll just break it up and get rid of it that way, than to give it to anybody else. The sad part of it is, this evacuation, what they did was the forty years of theIsseisworking, toiling hard, working hard, it just gone down the drain. Everything was taken away. So a lot of that, the people don't realize that, because when they gave out the redress money of $20,000, people on the outside thought, "Well, that's great, they got $20,000." But what they didn't realize is all the things that, personal things that they lost for the, from the forty years of hard work. That you could never get back.
AD: Where, where were you, were you taken to an assembly center?
GA: Yeah. When... when, after we disposed of everything, everything but our little personal things, we were given a family number on which we had put on all our packages and the suitcases so that it wouldn't get lost. And we assembled at our pick-up point and we were put on convoys and buses, and we, a convoy went all the way into Puyallup. And the Puyallup ground is, as it stands now, the fairgrounds is a real nice place. But at that time, there was three lots which they used as a parking lot for the fair. And what they did was to build barracks in every one of those. Rows and rows of makeshift barracks that would house some eight thousand of us. And the fairgrounds itself was labeled as Area D, and as we went inside, I could look and see up on the grandstand the machine guns and spotlights and soldiers manning the guns up there. And that was one of the depressing feeling I had, is, "Wow, we must be an 'enemy alien' now." And we, as we walked in, they closed the gates with barbed wire all around it and along the top. And so that was very depressing to know that, that they can do that to a citizen of this country.
AD: Did you consider yourself an American or Japanese? Which one?
GA: American first, Japanese next. Because we were born here, and we, I was born, raised, and was schooled here in Seattle, other than the four years that were taken away. And every day when we were small, we used to pledge allegiance to the flag. Not every day, but every Friday when we had a, what they referred to as assembly.

AD: What, what did, how did you feel? If you considered yourself American --
GA: Oh, that's right.
AD: -- how did it feel to be treated as an "enemy alien"?
GA: Well, let's put it this way: how would you feel if you were, you had your rights taken away? Well, that's exactly what I felt, because I'm an American first. And then -- because I hadn't been in Japan, only time was when I was small, when I was about five years old, we would up going there for about a month and a half. My parents had, had plans of sending my brother and me and leaving us over in Japan to have the grandparents raise us because the money stretched a little further over there than here. Therefore, they planned to send us over there, and what happened was we just couldn't get along with the kids over there. My brother just got in a fight with everybody, and within one, one week, he was tossed out of the school. They told him, "We don't want you." And so fortunately, they turned us back and we came home, and we haven't gone back since. I guess we left a bad impression. [Laughs]
