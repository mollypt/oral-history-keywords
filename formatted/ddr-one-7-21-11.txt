[This transcript is a translation of the original Japanese text.]
BB: I would like to ask you to tell us about what it was like before the war. When did you hear about the Manchurian Incident? Did you know about what was happening between Japan and China before the World War II?
MO: Something was going on. War against China started around 1938, did it? That was the second Sino-Japan war. My husband was deployed to the north area of China during the war. The Great East Asia War against the U.S. started in December, I believe, when my husband was still stationed in China and I was working. I remember it was very dark around 4 in the afternoon on my way home from work. It took me about fifteen minutes to walk home from the nearby station. I didn't see a single light on the way. We got the news that the war started right away. It was announced on the radio. Televisions were not around yet. I knew the war was on as I heard the news on the radio. I left the station in the dark and didn't quite know which way to go. It was completely dark. I had a general sense of direction, but it felt like walking in a cloud. [Laughs] I remember that. It was terrifying. We didn't get accurate news reports back then. News about the U.S. was always tied to victories of Japan. We won. We attacked Pearl Harbor. We won. We launched attacks somewhere. Another victory. They kept feeding us with all those victory news, but no one knew exactly what actually was going on. We had all the lights off at night in case U.S. troops were to come to air raid the area. All the lights were off. We used to have some lights here and there, in a shrine or on the street, to give us enough sense of direction to navigate. It was pitch-dark. It went on for a while. We were still told that Japan was winning, but no one knew the truth.
BB: And your father and mother...
MO: Right. I didn't have any contact with them. My father passed away in April in 1943. The notice took six months to arrive, and I received it in October through the Red Cross. It came through Geneva. It was sent from the States through Geneva to Japan. I found out my father died, but didn't find out what else was going on until the war ended. A soldier from the occupation forces came over to deliver a letter to let us know the family was doing okay, and that is when I finally found out. I asked him to take my letter to my family, but I don't know if it was delivered or not. They were still screening letters on their end. I don't know if it got through. [Laughs]
BB: When did you hear about the Sino-Japan War and the conflict in Manchuria? Did your husband talk about it?
MO: He didn't talk about it. He probably didn't want to. I didn't hear anything about the war from him.
BB: Were you worried about him?
MO: I was worried. [Laughs] We sent letters to each other. They were always opened and censored though. We couldn't write about anything sensitive. He sent me letters, and I sent him letters.
BB: What did the news tell you? What did they say about China, from the Japanese point of view...
MO: We didn't hear a lot about it. They were announcing Japan's victory in the big war, but they did not broadcast any defeats. We heard all about this and that troop marking a victory somewhere. No bad news was broadcast. We heard about people killed in a war somewhere through our friends and other people around us, but that was pretty much all.
BB: Did you hear about the Pearl Harbor attack?
MO: Yes, I did. The news came in right away. Victory, victory. We were celebrating our victory in Japan. [Laughs] Everyone was excited. We went to see a movie shortly after.
BB: Was it a movie about Pearl Harbor?
MO: Yes, we saw it.
BB: Did you?
MO: Yes.
BB: Did you see more war movies after that?
MO: I didn't really. I didn't like them. [Laughs] Being in a war is tough. I do hope it will not happen again. I remember how hard it was and how much people suffered.
BB: You must have been very weary.
MO: I was.
BB: You were worried about your husband.
MO: I was.
BB: And you were worried about your parents and your children.
