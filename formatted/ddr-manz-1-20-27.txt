AL: Also, back in that same era in the mid-'50s, I'm curious if the, the taunting that you received back when you first came back, I mean, did the climate, how that changed either with your age as you got older, or maybe with the times, going from the '40s to the '50s. In high school, did you feel accepted?
TO: Oh, yeah. Well, we all ran the place, and as I said, East L.A. was, it was a center for immigrant flows, historically.
AL: What percentage would you guess were Japanese Americans?
TO: We had a good concentration at Roosevelt High School, and they'd been there for, you know, I mean, if people were evacuated out of there, so they were there since the '30s. Yeah, Roosevelt was a very good place for Japanese Americans. Sort of like you're in charge. [Laughs]
AL: Did you hang mostly with Japanese Americans?
TO: No, I've never been that way. Well, that's probably... I've been very unlike most Japanese Americans. Japanese Americans tend to... kind of a village clumps, people were, liked to be in groups, and I've always been sort of a loner and an individual, and maybe it's this little girl walking out of the house and wandering around, as a little girl, but I had... my best friend, actually, I just saw her in New York, one of them was a Jewish girl who also went to UCLA, was always saying, "You're as smart as me, we're going together, okay?" But meanwhile, this kind of support that you need when you have these teachers shunting you into commercial courses.
AL: So what was your parents' reaction when they found out you were going to college?
TO: Oh, they were, by this time, it's great, other than, "Rose, nobody's going to marry you." And my father's fear was, who's going to hire you... if you don't study something like nursing, who's going to hire you? If you can, you go serve your own, and that's what all professions did, whether you were lawyers or doctors or dentists, you could go serve your own. But when you're studying to expect to be hired by a public institution, you may not [inaudible]. There were about two Japanese American girls from Roosevelt High School that went to UCLA before us, to study to become P.E. teachers. Big bunch of folks became P.E. teachers out of Roosevelt, because again, it was the gym teachers who could see, who were leaders both, you know, Latinos and all of us, and a good number of us became gym teachers. We went to UCLA and SC.
AL: How long were you a teacher?
TO: Seven years.
AL: Seven years, so that would put you up to the early '60s?
TO: Up to, yeah, up to late '60s, when I went to law school.
AL: Oh, late '60s. And somewhere in there, you acquired your dog-sitter?
TO: My what?
AL: Your dog-sitter, Tommy? [Laughs]
TO: Oh, my dog-sitter. [Laughs]
