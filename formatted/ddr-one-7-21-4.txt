BB: You lived in Japan for another ten years after the war.
MO: Yes, we did.
BB: How was your life in Japan for the ten years?
MO: It was rough. [Laughs] It was just very hard. When we went through the air raid, I had two children with me. Our first and second children. Bomb shells were blasting all over outside, and we had to flee. It was very hard though because we didn't know where to go for safety. There were some safe spots in the mountains, but it was hard to go there with children. I just put comforters over them and putgetaclogs on their feet. [Laughs] I knew they were dropping incendiaries. They were not explosive. I thought we could survive as long as the house was standing. I protected our two children, and we managed to survive the air raid. Our neighborhood was the only area that survived. We had about forty houses standing. Everything else around us was burned down. I was very grateful that our house was still there. It's strange, but I didn't feel scared when the air raid was on. I was so tense. My legs started to shake when it was over and the planes were completely out of sight. [Laughs] I never forget that.
BB: How about your children? Was Koichi born during the war?
MO: He was not born yet, and I had only Yoshiko and Noriko. Still only two girls. We lived in the middle of the city. My uncle invited me to come to live in his big house in the countryside because it was dangerous in the city. That was the house I grew up in. It was not very close to our house, but he invited us to come to live with him right away. We all ended up flying away again after the war though. [Laughs] Remembering the bombing still sends chills up on my spine when I look back. I didn't feel scared when it was blasting. Someone I knew was running away carrying her child on the back. A shell hit the child's back, and the child fell and died. The child died, and the parent survived and fled. That's what happened. The memories of the children and the town still terrify me. You could survive anything if you can manage to live through that. We didn't have anything to eat. Sugar was not available. We didn't even have sugar, soy sauce or salt. Of course we didn't have any rice.
BB: What did you eat?
MO: Potatoes and potato vines. Even soldiers were eating potato vines and maybe one rice ball. We made porridge for children, but it was all wheat and almost no rice. We all managed to survive though. [Laughs]
BB: Was Koichi born after the war?
MO: He was born after the war.
BB: He was.
MO: Yes.
BB: Was he eight or seven years old when he came to the States? Eight years old?
MO: He was seven years old, yes.
BB: He was. How about Noriko?
MO: She was fifteen. Noriko was fifteen, but she doesn't really remember... Koichi was a kindergartener. At school, he was seven... he was born in 1939, so he was around five in 1945. They don't remember what it was like during the war. I asked them, but they don't have any recollection. It was hard, and it continued to be hard for quite a while after the war. We had such hardships, but they didn't really see how hard it was for the parents. If I happen to mention that we didn't have [inaudible], and their response is, "Really? You didn't?" [Laughs] Children don't really feel the hardships. We were fortunate that my husband was working for a hospital for a long time. It was still a hard life, but he was working, and things were slowly becoming available after the war, unlike during the war when not a thing was available at all. Sometimes hospital patients gave us something sent from their family farm in the countryside. I was working for a school and received some gifts from the students' family. We had some gifts coming in. Our life eventually became a bit easier. I cannot tell you how hard it was during the war. We used parents' oldkimonoto make children'skimono. We repurposed and reshaped old clothes by sewing and knitting. We even sewedtabisocks because the government only provided us with one or two pairs a year. We had to be resourceful with a lot of sewing and knitting. We didn't even have anything to eat.
BB: Such experience makes you strong and able to survive in a harsh condition.
MO: Yes. Shipping route from the States to Japan was reestablished after the war, and my mother sent us a lot of items. We were fortunate that my mother was in the States. She sent usudonnoodles, rice, sugar and so on. [Laughs] It was a tremendous help. Our children were still very young.
BB: You got to get hold of her right after the war.
MO: Yes, I was able to.
