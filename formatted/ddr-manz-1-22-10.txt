AL: What was life like for your family between December and February of 1942? Before the executive order, what was, what was life like for your family?
YK: Well, it wasn't too bad because we didn't have too much contact with the Caucasian community. We, then we had a curfew imposed upon us, that was just before the executive order. But through January, February, before the curfew, my older brother -- I'm sorry, my wife's older brother, after he was discharged from the army, decided to get married. So he and a wife got married. I think they went to Catalina on a honeymoon before the curfew. So I said, "Well, we better get married, too, because we don't know what, where we were gonna be." So I took our mother to my wife's family's place, and got her permission, the father was interned already. So we got permission to get married.
AL: When did you get married, what date?
YK: So we got married on April 27th, which was one week before evacuation to Manzanar. So we had contact with the judges at the civic center downtown L.A. This Broadway was the center of justices of peace, all the justices had their offices there, so we went to one of them. We had a three-day wait for the blood tests, so we got to the judge's room, had the wedding, walked to the Biltmore Hotel on Fifth and Graham for our wedding luncheon.
AL: Were you already engaged before that?
YK: Well, sort of. So we came home from the luncheon and spent our honeymoon at Olympic Hotel. It was, the Olympic Hotel came up in downtown L.A., just across from [inaudible] church. In the meantime, while we were in the hotel, Sue, my mother, Midori, the youngest boy, were at the Union church signing up to go to Manzanar. And my wife, naturally, being from Hollywood, said she thought she would be with her folks at Santa Anita. But my brother said, "No, you can't separately," because my older brother had been in Manzanar helping the volunteers put the finishing touches on the bungalows in Manzanar. So we got sent to Manzanar, and that's another story.
