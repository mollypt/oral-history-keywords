JA: Tell me about... it was a long time afterwards but at some point the government finally got enough courage to say, "We did wrong," and to pay out a check.
AM: Oh, yes, uh-huh.
JA: How did you feel about that?
AM: Well, you know, for me, I was still going to school and things like that, so the things that I lost are the, well, the freedom and things like that. Monetarily, probably not that much, but for these old first-generation Japanese like my father and others, they lost so much, you know, and I really feel sorry for them. No amount of money would ever compensate for it, for what they went through. So, well, I guess my father set out to record the camp life so this kind of thing will never happen again. And maybe -- I hope this, all of what he did will prevent things like that to happen again. Sometimes I get called to school to show some of the photographs and talk about camp life to the students, and what we had to go through and things like that. So things, so I could see myself how important these photographs are to show people what actually happened.
JA: Do you think people have learned?
AM: I hope so, especially right now with this, whatever, that... the 9/11 incidents and things like that, people are getting arrested. I just hope that they won't do a mass evacuation like they did with us, and I don't think they will because of what happened to the Japanese. And I think people, well, a lot of people don't know anything about the evacuation, but schools are teaching that, some of the schools, I noticed, so I get called to go to the school. So, I think people are much -- well, some of the people like Daniel Inouye, Senator Inouye, he, I'm sure he's doing a lot in Washington to caution people about, you know, don't do mass evacuation and things like that.
JA: When you went back home, you had a home to go to. Do you know about other people who may have had more difficulty getting resettled?
AM: Oh, yes. Now, I remember going to a trailer court over here in Burbank near the Lockheed aircraft, and there were a lot of trailers which were empty because the defense work slowed down so the people who lived there were gone so some of the Japanese were put in there because there was such a big housing shortage. Now, we had about -- my father had a two-, two-bedroom den. It wasn't a very big house but we had about fifteen to twenty people living in there, and we would have to eat in shifts until eventually they found a place to rent, but then -- and then I remember going to a church. They strung ropes across the big hall and they hung blankets and they divided it into a lot of little sections for families to sleep in, things like that. I saw those, you know, things that happened. I didn't take any photo, I didn't have the heart to take a photograph like that so I didn't take any pictures like that, but I saw those things.
JA: And I imagine it was hard for a lot of people to find work, too.
AM: Yes, I guess -- well, I guess so because of the slow-down in the economy at that time right after the war, yeah.
