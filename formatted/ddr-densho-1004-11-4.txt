gky: Now, it was still in camp that you were asked to volunteer for what later became Merrill's Marauders?
GH: After graduation, we were transferred to Fort Snelling. We were waiting for overseas assignment, and it was during that period they did come around and they asked me if I would volunteer. And all I knew at the time was that it was a dangerous and hazardous mission. What they failed to tell us was that according to War Department estimate, they had anticipated 85 percent casualty.
gky: But they didn't tell you that.
GH: No.
gky: So why did you volunteer for this assignment that was dangerous and hazardous?
GH: Right. It's war. There was work to be done. I said, "Let's get it over with."
gky: It's just hard for me to understand. Certainly there's a different feeling in the country, but to...
GH: Well, it's war and there's a -- it's difficult, yes. But I felt that there was work to be done, and I wanted to do my share. But in my prayer was if I were in combat, I said please, I did not wish to meet my relative or my classmates. But, fortunately, the unit in which we fought were from Kyushu. I was from Nagano prefecture.
gky: Did you think about your family at all?
GH: Oh, yes, constantly.
gky: But when you volunteered for this mission, did you think well, I may never see my mom and dad again?
GH: Well, yes. Yeah. That's war.
gky: What was stated to you as the mission of Merrill's Marauders? What was stated to you as your mission?
GH: Our mission, as I recall, the Burma Road, which is the lifeline, land lifeline to China, was occupied by the Japanese. And in order to keep China in the war, they had to get, assist and aid to China. So they established a new road called Ledo Road, and from Ledo, it's in northern India, to complete the Ledo to connect with the old Burma Road. In order to accomplish this -- and there was the 18th Division, the Kurumeshi-dan, which occupied Northern Burma, the [inaudible] Valley. It was our mission to remove the 18th Division. Our mission was a long-range penetration. We would go behind enemy lines, and while we destruct the enemy's communication and supply line, the Americans trained Chinese units made the frontal attack.
gky: So you knew that you were going to be infiltrating, going behind enemy lines?
GH: Yes.
gky: How did the term "Merrill's Marauders," the name "Merrill's Marauders" come about?
GH: It wasTimeas I understand it,Timemagazine correspondent Shipley. One day was riding with Merrill while they were making a river crossing, and Shipley told General Merrill, "I think 5307th, Composite Unit is a honorable name," but he thought maybe Merrill's Marauder would be more appropriate. And the general said, "Huh." And that was it. That's how we got the name Merrill's Marauders.
gky: Can you describe to me a little bit about what it was like being out there in...
GH: Well, I kicked myself many times, because during the training and during maneuver, I was exposed to K-rations for the first time. Now, the K-ration, I think I was allergic to the preservative. So after I ate the K-ration I broke out in hives. My lips were so -- I had bumps on my head, bumps all over my body. So the following day, I did report to sick leave, or sick call, and the medical officer said, "Grant, you're allergic to K-rations." I also, during the maneuver, jumped from high ground and the butt of the rifle struck my elbow and I had a chipped elbow, chipped bone in my elbow. I was called in by the battalion medical officer who said, "Grant," the general said I am unfit for combat. So be it. I said, "Captain, Captain McLaughlin," I said, "I've trained for six months in school, I've trained for this," and we were paired -- there were two of us, as a team. I might add that the total number of Merrill's Marauders to be exactly 2,997 or 3,000. And the unit was divided into three battalions, and each battalion was divided into two columns, or combat team. TwoNiseiswere assigned to each column or combat team and, of course, they were teamed one strong in Japanese, one strong in English. For me to pull out I would suddenly put my teammate, Eddie Mitsukado, in trouble. So I insisted. And the surgeon said, "You have to face the consequences," and I said, "I am determined to go in." So the battalion medical officer was very considerate. I remember there was, all together, on three occasions, he personally came to me to see how I was coming along, and he would say, "Don't eat this, and don't eat that." And I followed his order, but by the end of the day you're so hungry that it went out the window.
