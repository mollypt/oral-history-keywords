AL: What   about your sister, coming back, do you... what do you recall of her   transition, because she was the oldest? She would have   been...
TO: Well,   you know, my sister, a little bit younger than a lot of the women   that never got married because, you know, the eligible bachelors   were, many of them were killed, boyfriends were killed, and others   were off in the service. And so there's a lot of women that didn't   get married, just a little older than my sister.
AL: Did   she not get married?
TO: My   sister got married, and had children, and... but also I feel sorry   for her, and I'm sure she has a lot of resentment, and that, smart   as she was, she only went to a couple of years of college, got a   bookkeeping degree, got a job at the Good Samaritan Hospital, and   worked until she got married. Because the family needed help, and   so she'd turn over her check, and everything was essential. So she,   she didn't get to pursue the kind of advanced degrees that I have   been able to do, being the youngest. So it was kind of unfair for   people of her generation.
AL: Did   she ever talk about the camp experience, or does she now?
TO: No,   she doesn't. She's... I think she's enjoying her life now, and it's   her life. Her generation's experience very different than mine. She   was raised to fit a role, and she carried out that role; she was a   good wife and mother. When her husband passed, and her kids were   all in college, finally, there was a time to say, "Who am I? What   do I want to do?" And she's doing it. And, you know, I'm just eight   years younger, but just a whole different kind of experience,   where...
AL: It   makes a big difference.
TO: Yes,   we can make choices that she couldn't.
AL: What   did, what did George grow up to be?
TO: He   became, well actually, he went to college and then he volunteered   for the army, and he was in Germany, and he loved it during   peacetime. And he wanted to stay, and he was dating a German girl   and all, and my father would have nothing to do with that. It's   kind of sad. Made him come back, got his master's degree, and   worked for a while in social work, but he didn't like it, and so he   studied and picked up another degree. And then he ended up working   as an engineer for the County of Los Angeles.
AL: When   your, when your brother and sister got married, did they marry   Caucasians or Japanese Americans?
TO: My   sister married a Japanese American, and my brother married a   Caucasian.
AL: Did   that cause any sort of ripple in your family?
TO: Oh, it   did for a while. It's much earlier than the cousins that I had just   showed you photos of.
[Interruption]
TO: We   were talking about my brother; my brother married a Caucasian, and   while my parents had some difficulties accepting her, that when the   grandchildren are born, there's a way of bringing them together and   then things were okay.
AL: Do you   know about what year they were married? Was that in the '40s,   '50s?
TO: My   brother?
AL:   Yeah.
TO: No,   no, no, my brother got married probably around '60, 1960.
AL: Okay.   I don't know what year they changed the law that, the   anti-miscegenation laws.
TO: Yeah,   I think that in California it was a lot earlier.
AL: Yeah,   it would have had to be.
