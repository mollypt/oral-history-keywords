SE: You know, that was a kind of a sad situation where I think that many of the people were, it was some ruse because there was no real reason to take all those hotels down, well, not quite skid row hotels, but the hotels that the Japanese were able to run. They said that those buildings were needed for wartime housing, and it was never used for that. However, when the Japanese lost their leases and were not able to obtain another one, because not knowing any of this, Dad lost the lease to this... it was called Boston Hotel at the time that he got it. And he had run it for one year, and his lease was up, and so he went down the street, and at the Temport Hotel, which is on the corner of Riverside and Washington, he talked to the man that owned the building, and the man that was running it no longer wanted to do that business, so Dad had made all the arrangements and talked to the gentleman and everything was fine. And... but during that time, my oldest sister married a Caucasian man who was a Pentecostal minister, and Dad thought, well, because of this wartime, maybe he'll give him a job running that hotel. So he sent him down to City Hall to get the license. And Dad gave him the money, but I think he felt proud that he had this license in his hand and he took it up to his room. And when Safety Commissioner Coburn heard about it, he sent his deputies to my brother-in-law's room to retrieve the license. And because he already had paid the fee and had the license in his hand, he didn't have to give it to the deputies but he returned it to them. And so when Mr. Blair heard about it, Mr. Blair, the one that I was working for, he was so angry, he said that they have no business taking it away after it was already approved and he had it. So he asked for a special meeting with the city council and he went with, he took me as an interpreter for my dad, we appeared before the city council. And they took a vote, and all the councilmen said they didn't see why Dad couldn't have his business license. But when it came to Mr. Coburn, he spoke up, and he said, "We don't need any more Jap money in downtown Spokane." And with the one dissenting vote, they couldn't have the license. And Mr. Blair spoke up and said that Dad was a hotel businessman, Dad had done business in Seattle, and a reputable man, and there has not been anything to say that he was disloyal or anything, so didn't see why. But he said, no, one dissenting vote and he couldn't have the license. And so Dad was so angry, but he said everything was approved and was all set and he was going to have this building. So he was in his sixties but, and he doesn't speak much English, but he bought a bus ticket and went to Chicago. He didn't know anybody in Chicago but he said, "It's because this is such a small city." He says, "I'm going to a big place." He took a bus to Chicago, and about two weeks after he was there, he called my brother and says, "Pack up and get rid of the things at the hotel." He says, "Come over, I've found the place for us." And so that's how come the family moved to Chicago.
