gky: Let's see. What happened with the Merrill's Marauders? How long was the mission and how long in one...
GH: Actually, we went in in late February and...
gky: Can you also tell what year it was?
GH: Yes. It was in late February, 1944, that we entered Burma. And after the battle of Nhphum Ga, I was evacuated. I might just give you my experience. I was told to report to the air strip, and when I got there, what it was was a rice field, and I saw them tying L-5, which was the liaison planes the size of a Piper Cub. They were tying the airplane to a tree with the parachute rope. And I got on and the pilot said, "We will not take off until sundown because we're still behind enemy lines." And at sundown, they started the motor and the takeoff is so short what they did was tie the plane to the tree and run the rpm to the desired point. And, of course, the plane was just jumping up and down. And finally the pilot stuck his hand out and a machete went down and the plane just took off. All you could see was a very close call because I could see the bamboo. [Laughs] And we meandered, and we went back. And after one month of treatment, I flew back.
gky: I know they were talking about this, and it all seems very, almost like from a movie or something. I mean, just imagining it in my head, but how did you feel? What did you feel like inside when all this was happening to you?
GH: Well, it's scary, and you're hungry, you're tired, you're filthy. And that is war.
gky: What is the thing that is the hardest for you thinking about, thinking about those days? What is the hardest thing about remembering them?
GH: Just the loss of friends, and we did have casualties. Now, you ask, why was he shot and why did -- why were you spared?
gky: Yeah. That must be -- it must be very hard because you had quite a few friends who were shot. When you think about those days, also, it seems like I'll see you all at reunions and there's a certain camaraderie there that you can't really describe. You can't really describe.
GH: Well, you know, you know Roy Matsumoto. He was our hero. So I'm usually with him, but I can hear his friends say, "Roy, I'm glad to see you. Hey, if it wasn't for you I wouldn't be here."
gky: How did Merrill's Marauders break up?
GH: Actually, I don't, I can't recall. I guess it was around the 10th of August. They exercise their "provisional" and they just disbanded. And I was flown back to India and I was assigned to SEATIC, South-East Asia Translation and Interrogation Center, and there I was also assigned to the British Royal Air Force as a translator. It was very simple. There was no parade, no nothing; they said, "That's it."
gky: How long did you serve in Merrill's Marauders?
GH: From October of 1943 through August 10, 1944.
gky: When you think about it, what do you think about them having told you that it was a dangerous and hazardous mission?
GH: Well, when the battle of Myitkyina was over, as far as the Merrill's Marauders was concerned, out of the original 3,000 men, there were only about 200 that was able to carry on for another day or week.
gky: You mean that many of you were decimated by being sick or...
GH: The majority of them were sick: typhus, malaria, dysentery.
gky: Boy, that's a huge number, I mean, that were sick. Did you, you know a lot of people think that war, you get shot, or it is something of a physical wound like that, but a lot of you were sick. The Japanese were also very sick. Can you describe the prisoners to me, the people you took prisoners?
GH: Well, you know, all our ground transportation was horses and mules. All the supplies, ammunition were air dropped, so we did have unpredictable drops, because when you're behind enemy lines, a drop could give away your position or weather could delay your drop, the terrain could delay your drop; so there were many days we went hungry. But at least we had some hope of air drop. For the Japanese, we would cut their supply line so they were desperate. When the POWs were interrogated, many were out of food for a number of days. They were eating off the land, eating bamboo shoots, and they had very little medical aid. So quite a few of them were in very bad shape when they were taken POW.
gky: Seems a surprise that Japanese could still be fighting when so many of them had tropical diseases, beriberi, or dysentery, malaria, typhus. There's a lot of things out there.
GH: Yeah, but there again, it's war.
gky: Were the prisoners very often so sick they needed medical attention before you could talk to them, or before they would talk?
GH: In my case, I think I had about half a dozen that I wanted to have the medical officer look at them before I interrogated them.
