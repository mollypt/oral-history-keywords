AL: I'd like to back up just a little bit and talk about any memories that you have before, before the war, like memories of your life in your home. I know you were just a little kid when that happened. Do you have any memories pre-camp?
TO: I don't know exactly whether they are memories or I remember what my mother told me about myself, and that I was a happy little girl who would wake up in the morning and, and go out the door and not come back 'til I was hungry. [Laughs] And then I was, you know, I just visited with neighbors, and the postman and whatever, and I was very different than her two other children, older brother and sister, they pretty much stayed around the house and I was, she'd like to say that I was her adventurous one.
AL: When was your brother born?
TO: He's two years older than me.
AL: Okay, so that'd be 1936, and what is his name?
TO: His name is George; we lost him last December.
AL: That would be December 2001? I mean, six months ago?
TO: Yes, yes. And I can tell you a little about, I think, his generation. We all came out of the experience affected in different ways. But I think that the... so many -- and I believe my brother was one of them -- so many of the young men were, were just very angry, and didn't have, as women have, the ability to deal with some of the repressed feelings of anger and oppression, and I think women find in different ways to be able to deal with some of those issues much better. And I would just tell you that if you don't release, the anger finds a way of expressing itself, and in clouding one's happiness in life. I know that so much of that residual frustration sometimes would surface in situations where all this accumulated anger erupts, and I assume this happened with otherNisei, second-generation Japanese. My brother was a little younger than that, mostly, but at some point that I have observed...
AL: You said there were four kids in your family. Who is the fourth one?
TO: I had a little brother who was born in camp. And it's very sad, because my mother told me at one time that she sought to abort him, and that the women used various kinds of techniques, and because she did not, they didn't know what was going to happen next. And but anyway, she was not successful, and he was born retarded.
AL: They believed because of her attempt to abort him?
TO: Yes.
AL: And was she pregnant when she went to camp, or was this during camp?
TO: No, no, during camp. And from, she didn't tell me until, you know, when I was grown up, but she said that there were a lot of women doing this.
AL: Is he still alive?
TO: No, he's not.
AL: And what was his name?
TO: His name was Takeshi.
AL: Takeshi? How would you spell that?
TO: T-A-K-E-S-H-I.
