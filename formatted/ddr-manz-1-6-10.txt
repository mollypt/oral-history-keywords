JA: Tell me a little bit more about the sports, I mean, who would you play?
AM: Well, some teams were formed by where they came from, some of them by, some of them would form a club and make a team that way. It was mostly from the area wherever they came from. Well, there was a group of all men that formed, my father was playing on that team. Of course, they were horsing around most of the time. But their team name was really crazy; it was called the Shochu Gumi, which means...shochuis this alcohol that people brewed in camp. [Laughs] So it's more or less people who knows something aboutshochuor drinks it, and so they called themselves that group. I remember one night after coming back from a dance, I was walking by my mess hall... I don't know if I should say anything like this, but anyway, the smokestack was red hot. I thought, "My gosh, I wonder what's going on." So I looked inside the kitchen and I see several men in there, they got the oil stove going full blast and they were distilling this stuff. [Laughs] They'd get figs and things like that and ferment it and... and I saw this one man with a little cup where the copper tubing was dripping some of this alcohol. And he's tasting it and he said, "Oh," he says, "this one is good," you know, he was saying. [Laughs] Well, anyway, alcohol was prohibited in camp, but then so, well, when you have to have it, they'll make it somehow. [Laughs]
JA: Who did we talk with who made distillery equipment?
Male voice: Oh, Frank Kikuchi.
JA: Frank Kikuchi's father used to make distillery equipment.
AM: Oh, yeah? [Laughs]
JA: You told me when we talked once before about sometimes playing teams from outside of camp.
AM: Oh, yes. We had a high school football team and well, we would play among ourselves but then you wanted to play with somebody, some other team. So I guess the school was able to get some of the schools, high schools in Lone Pine or Independence to come and play. Well, they had about four or five games there as I recall, and we were never visiting team. We would always have to be the home team because we weren't allowed to leave the camp. So all the football games which they, we played were all in camp, and, boy, you know, the high schools in Lone Pine and Independence, those fellows are big. They were twice the size of these Japanese Americans, but the only advantage we had was we were fast on our feet. So we just, just about almost ran a circle around them because they were so fast with their feet. Well, we were all pretty satisfied because we won every game, and I think the camp people were very happy to see the team win the game like that all the time. But I took some photographs of the football games. I didn't play it but I was able to just watch it or take photographs. But, gosh, you know, the Japanese Americans, they would try to block the opponent, but they just ran right over 'em because they're so big. But once we got the ball we kind of ran, outran them. So... it kind of gave the boys encouragement that they could win the game even though they're so small.
[Interruption]
JA: I'd like to have you tell me about one other kind of advantage. You told me before that you thought in addition to being fast on their feet, you had another advantage. You already told me that you were always the home team.
AM: Oh, yes, uh-huh. Yeah, well, since we couldn't leave the camp, we were not allowed to leave the camp to go play at a high school outside of Manzanar, so we always had to be the home team. So... well, in that way, we got a lot of support from the people watching the game, I guess, that's one advantage. [Laughs]
JA: Would you say it was a home team advantage?
AM: Yeah, I think so, we had the home team advantage, yeah.
