AL: What about your mother, what is your mother's name?
YK: Komika, K-O-M-I-K-A. I really don't know what that meant.
AL: And where was she from?
YK: She was from probably that same village where my father came from, and she came as a picture bride. And in those days they had hats and long dresses and... we have a picture of her and the husband, so...
AL: Do you know when your parents were born?
YK: No, I have no idea.
AL: Did they know each other in Japan before he came over?
YK: No, I don't think so. You know, when they sent over a picture to woo the lady in Japan, the men would send all the handsome, nice-looking, tall picture of themselves, usually borrowed from friends. We heard many stories like that, however.
AL: It's like Internet dating, huh?
YK: Yeah, it is.
AL: Buyer beware. [Laughs]
YK: Yeah.
AL: When did your father come to this country?
YK: He originally went to Hawaii, 'cause that's where they needed labor, pineapple, sugar cane, any kind of field labor, I guess. And so I don't know how long he stayed there, I guess bunch of bachelors making up a gang of workers. So then they came to Hollywood where there were jobs, domestic, butler, butler... and because mainly men learned to drive, they were chauffeurs in Hollywood.
AL: Is that what your father did?
YK: No, he did domestic work.
AL: What, do you know what port of entry he came in through?
YK: I think it was San Francisco. Many... otherwise they would have settled in Seattle, because that's where most of the mainland women settled, in Seattle. And they ended up as domestics, and...
AL: Did, so they, your mom was a picture bride, do you know when she came over?
YK: Well, I don't know, a few years after they, they had, men had come to the mainland.
AL: Did she also come through San Francisco?
YK: Yes.
AL: And so their first home was down here?
YK: Yes. I really didn't know where my oldest brother was born.
