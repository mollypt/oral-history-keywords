AI: And do you have any memory of when you first realized the difference between you, as a Japanese American, and the Caucasian peers?
CN: I think we were made to realize that at a very early age because we spokeNihongoat home and we were learning English. So I know I felt different, and I think we all did. We were well aware of that, because our parents didn't look like other kids' parents, and they didn't speak English. And I remember we were asked what we had for breakfast, and we talked about having rice, havinggohan. And the teacher saying, "Well, you must have toast and eggs," and this kind of thing.
AI: Was that difficult? Did you have some difficult or painful times over that difference?
CN: I must have. I must have because I always felt that I was different. It did help going to school with all theseNisei,Niseikids because you weren't the only one. But we were made to feel different. At least, I felt it. And so, and at home, I don't know about other families, but in my family I had to speak Japanese.
