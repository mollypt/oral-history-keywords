SE: You know, we never did. I don't know why we didn't. We were too busy with everything, I think, that was going on, I don't know. And so it's sad, but it's the same way everywhere. I talked to my sisters and my friends, and they said no, they never even talked about it to their kids. I don't know whether we were ashamed that we were treated the way we were or not, but I know that many of the second generation young people got after the folks and said, "Why didn't you have gumption enough to oppose it?" But it was difficult. There was too much to... we weren't strong enough a group to be able to protest anything. And all these things were, the law and these things were set up for us. And like the JACL, they were criticized, but like my father had said, it was best to cooperate with the government and do things peaceably so you don't have any big problems. And so I think that's how come things went so well is because this was a government edict and you had to abide by it. So we felt that that was a necessary part of our living here. I don't know, other than that, I don't know why we didn't object. I suppose quietly by ourselves, we can't see why this is happening to us, but it was one of the things that the government had said and so we were going to do what we were told.
AD: Do you think it's important now to talk about it with your kids or with your grandkids? Do you think it was important for them to know?
SE: Yeah, it was important for them to know, and maybe we should have said something. But I don't know why we didn't. We never talked about it. Maybe when some of us, our friends and us, when we got together we talked, but see, we weren't with the same friends that we had over there when we came here, because we came separately. Now, people in camp probably when they were together they talked, because they were together. But here we were not among people that were moved, so I think maybe that's all the more reason why we didn't talk about it.
AD: Do you talk about it much now?
SE: Not too much, huh? I haven't told you too much about things. I don't know. I don't know why we didn't, it just, we had other things that were pressing like keeping them clothed and fed, keeping the household going and the business problems and stuff. I guess maybe those are the reasons why, every day, keeping the house warm and finding clothes for them, and what to feed them. I think those things were foremost in our minds. I don't know, it's just something that we just didn't talk about.
AD: What do you hope for the next generation? When you think about what you went through, what are you hopeful for for the...
SE: The generations to come? That they be aware of these things before it comes. And so that's why the JACL and the groups were trying to help with Arabs and people like that, that when something comes up, then they feel that they're going to be ostracized or some things happening. And so we feel that it's something that should never happen again. And that a person, if they're willing to be put into prison or whatever, that they need to be heard and tried for whatever reason, that they should be removed. But it was difficult, you realize, that there was such a thing of hysteria because this was the first time anything happened with the United States to have enemy come into their own land and be invaded that way. But I'll tell you that, why, I don't know, we just have to be vigilant, and we should remember that things can happen when you get people all excited about something and they feel that their safety is endangered, they may, again, have something like this happen. But I don't think so because there was quite a bit brought to the surface about why it didn't need to have to happen. Well, some of... I think some of the reasons why this went to strong is because the Japanese farmers down in California, they were doing so well. And I think a lot of the people were jealous and felt that this is a good chance to get them out. Because like our family, they just left, my husband's folks left everything as it was and just left and never went back. So they had things there that belonged to them, and we did, too, but Dad, we were close enough to Seattle so we had a hotel business. He lost that, but we had a house on Beacon Hill, and I think he was able to leave it with reliable people and so it was rented out. But we didn't get any money for it, but I think he got his house back. I don't really check with him on that.
AD: Seiko, when you think about... what do you think about having lived through World War II as a Japanese American? What are your thoughts on freedom and what it means to earn freedom and to be a free citizen?
SE: I'm not sure how to answer that.
AD: Well, I guess you answered a lot of that question, but when you have your rights, even though you weren't interned yourself, the Japanese Americans had some of their freedoms taken away.
SE: Taken away, uh-huh.
AD: That now, my generation, your daughter's generation, we enjoy those freedoms without question. Do you think at all about kind of the freedom, just the nature of freedom and what it means?
SE: Yeah, we have to keep abreast, know what's going on instead of blindly just going your own way, and take more interest in what's going on. And when it's time to... like the election time, know that, who's going to be a more... well, we can't say lenient and things, but it's just that, who really looks after us and our interests. But that's why, and we need to have people in government that understand some of the different peoples, and so I think that's why a lot of the, some of the young people are interested in getting into politics so that people of different nationalities and things can have equal rights and not be all of a sudden targeted as "enemy aliens" like. I don't know other than that. But it is important, though, that in school, that our young people can learn about what happened and what could happen so that be more aware of how they can prevent anything to happen to any, certain groups of people just because of your race and because we over here had no ideas what's going on over there because we have no connection, no interest, no real interest except in cultural, language and arts or something like that. Other than that, we have no interest in what they do politically, but we have to kind of keep aware, abreast of what's going on or these things can happen to groups. So it can't be, we can't just think of just ourselves, but it's all the other minorities and people that have problems.
