gky: You were talking about being in the army, or how you signed up for the Army Air Corps. What happened when you got put in protective custody and what did it mean?
GH: Well, I was transferred from Fort Lewis, Washington, to Jefferson Barracks, Missouri. On the way I said to myself, "I made a very good choice," because the army, in those days, did not have any gas masks and rifles to be issued. As a matter of fact, I recall one recruit asking his sergeant, he says, "Sarge, what do we do if we have a gas attack?" He said, "Men," with a straight face. He said, "Men, you will take your helmet, which will be issued when it becomes available, you dig a hole in the ground and you stick your nose in." So I also saw at the Fort, I heard the people saying, "Bang, bang, bang." I said, "My gosh, what is this?" And I looked and it was in the woods between two trees. They had a wire and on that wire was hanging a lettuce crate with a picture of a tank and the men on the ground with wooden guns were saying, "Bang, bang, bang." So when I was on my way to Jefferson Barracks, Missouri, I'd pat myself on the back and said, "I've made the right choice. I didn't want to be in the army that did not have rifle or the proper equipment." But to my surprise, when I arrived in Jefferson Barracks, Missouri, I was immediately taken to what they called "protective custody." There were altogether twenty-six of us confined to one barracks. And frankly, I didn't know exactly why we were put in there. I took it for granted at that time that we were being checked for our background. But after the war, I believe it was Aiko Herzig or Jack Herzig that went through the archives and they came across my name and they sent the article to me. It was an order and, according to this order, there was a case when aNiseiGI was undergoing a physical and a Caucasian took -- his action was such that they had to remove theNiseito another room to complete his physical, and that's where the protective custody comes in.
gky: So you were allNiseithat were taken to these barracks?
GH: Yes.
gky: And whose protection then? It was for your protection or was the protection of all the non-Japanese Americans?
GH: Well, I never thought of it that way. I thought because the GI was threatened, it was for our protection. But we were confined for forty days, and during that time, I do recall, we were free to receive letters and write letters. Many of the people, our friends, were being discharged. Can you imagine the men being sworn into the army and then they were discharged at the convenience of the government, and reclassified 4-C?
gky: But you were not?
GH: I was not. But, we were -- we didn't know what our status would be, but these rumors and actual letters were being exchanged.
gky: You must have felt a lot of uncertainty.
GH: Yes, a lot of uncertainty.
gky: How did you feel knowing that your parents were, by this point, had been taken to Tule Lake, Pinedale and then Tule Lake?
GH: Yes.
gky: And how'd you feel knowing that your parents were behind barbed wire and you on the other side?
GH: Yeah, very difficult. As a matter of fact, I had an occasion to visit Tule Lake and, as I entered the camp, it was in a deserted desert surrounded by barbed wire. There was a guard with a machine gun facing inward, not outward. The guard did take me halfway to the barracks. He pointed out and said, "That is the barrack." When I was at the barrack, it came as a shock to know my parents were removed from their comfortable home and placed in such a desolate area. It's very indescribable.
gky: And how did you feel? Here you are wearing the same uniform as the guard that's walking you to the barracks where your parents are living.
GH: I'm sorry?
gky: How did you feel knowing that you're wearing the same uniform as the guard walking you to the barracks?
GH: Right. I think I thought it was very courteous of him to at least walk me halfway and to point out the barrack. And it did leave some favorable impression.
gky: What did your parents say to you about the fact that you were in the army and they were in that camp?
GH: Well, I still remember my father saying, "These are very difficult times. You take care and do your best."
gky: So they really supported the fact that you were fighting for America?
GH: Yes.
gky: But you must have had some conflict about...
GH: It was very difficult, yes. And, you take the oath to uphold the Constitution and fight for freedom and liberty, and to find your parents and siblings in camp -- it was very difficult.
