JA: What were your first... what were the first things you noticed at Manzanar, the first day you were there?
AM: Okay, because we went by train. We boarded a train in Santa Fe station there. It's in the old Grand Central Station in Los Angeles, not the Union Station where it is now. We boarded a train there and then as soon as the train started to move, we were all told to pull down the blinds so we couldn't see outside, and it really went along real slowly. We must have left quite early in the morning, I forget what time it was, but we got to Lone Pine probably about four, four o'clock or something, and then by the time we reached Manzanar it was about five or five-thirty. Now, before we were there, my cousin, who came back from Japan on the last ship from Japan in 1941, '40 or... one of the last ships that came from Japan -- she volunteered to go to Manzanar as one of the workers. So she went probably in about February of that year, 1942, and she was there and she would write back and tell us what kind of weather it is and told us there's scorpions and it's windy and dusty. So with that in mind, we prepared ourselves, we bought heavy jackets and we bought leather boots which came up to our knees so in case we get, in case of rattlesnake bite or something so we'll be protected, we bought boots like that. And told us how windy it is so we bought goggles and hat, or helmet-like thing to cover our head and ears and everything. So we tried to make ourselves dustproof as possible, and when we got there it was about five-thirty or getting kind of, sun was setting.
Sure enough, there was a little wind blowing and there was dust, but it wasn't that bad at that time. But we were out, had to get in line and get checked off for if we had our inoculation shots and things like, we took our, our family took our shots before going into camp so we didn't have to take anymore shots -- typhoid shots. And then we lined up and got our peacoat and war surplus clothes and blanket, and then we were given a great big canvas bag and we didn't know what that was for and then so we asked them and they said, "Oh, you go over there, there's a stack of hay, you can just fill it up with your, fill that up with the hay and then that's your mattress." And I found out just a few years ago that it was Mr. Stewart's family that got all those hays, and he was wondering what it was for at the time, so I told him what happened, what we did and he says, oh, yeah, it kind of reminded him of the days when he was there as a youngster.
Well, we got into our barrack. We kind of set it up, tried to make it as comfortable as we can and went to sleep that night, and that night there was a pretty big windstorm. We could hear the wind noise and the dust -- we could almost just about hear the noise of the dust because it's hitting the side of the wall, and there was no drywall inside because... and also there was no linoleum on the floor and you could just almost smell the dust blowing in. The next morning when I got up, I looked at my mattress and right where my head was is still all white, but then the rest of it was all covered with sand. And so I thought, "Oh my gosh, I wonder how long we're going to have to stay here," and we have to do something to try to seal the walls so we don't get so much dust in. Well, we tried to stuff newspaper between the glass window so we won't... and then covered the knotholes on the floor with a can that we got from the mess hall, and then eventually they distributed drywall and we put the drywall up ourselves. That's when I learned how to use drywall, and so after we came back from camp we had no place to sleep, so... see, the house was still occupied by the tenant -- the people who rented it, so we put the drywall up in the garage and we slept in our garage for a while until they were able to find a place to move to. Well, and then eventually the linoleum crew came around and put the linoleum in for us. And that somewhat -- the drywall and the linoleum kind of helped a little bit, but still it's pretty hard to keep the dust from coming in.
JA: How many of you were living in that apartment?
AM: Let's see, our family, which was a family of five, and then the -- my cousin, she was not married yet then, and then another couple, so about eight people. So we had one of the larger apartments.
JA: There was room enough.
AM: Yes, uh-huh.
