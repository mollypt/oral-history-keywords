JA: Did you remember anything about the riot that happened there?
AM: No, I don't remember too much about it because I was about to go to the area and my father stopped me and he says, "Don't ever go over there, you just stay right here," and then I couldn't leave the place. And then one of my father's friends came running back, he was there, and he was really mad and he says one of the MPs shot somebody, and he was just pale, he was, you know, he was so scared. He came running back. But I was too young to know the inside story of what actually happened.
JA: Did you have to deal with the loyalty questions?
AM: Yes, I was... I was at the age where I had to go for the hearing, and I think at the very beginning, at the 27 and 28th question I said, 'no-no,' because to be detained in a camp like this and be asked that kind of question, I think that was, I thought that was ridiculous. So I said, 'no-no', and I went back to my father and I told him what I did. And my father says, "No, no, you shouldn't do that. You should have said 'yes' or something." And then a little while later, I got called for a second hearing, and so Mr. Merritt was doing the questioning both times, the first and the second time, and then so I said -- he asked me the same kind of question, "Do you forswear allegiance, be loyal to the United States?" I said, "Yes."
JA: What was the whole question, the first question?
AM: Let's see. "Do you forswear allegiance to Japan, and forswear allegiance to the United States?" or something like that, I can't remember. Anyway, it was about loyalty. So I said, "Yes." Then this next question was whether I would serve in the armed force. So I told Mr. Merritt, "You know, I don't think I would serve in the armed force. Maybe if I could serve in such a way that I don't have to go out on a battlefield I would go, but other than that I would say no," so I said 'yes-no'. And then another thing, too, if I said 'no-no', my father was also worried the fact that if I said 'no-no' then I would be sent to another camp and be separated from the family, and my father was so worried about a thing like that that he wanted the family to stay together during the duration. So I thought well, maybe I, I thought he was right, I should try to stay with the family so I wouldn't be separated. Of course, at that time, we didn't know we would be separated but there was some kind of a talk going on that people who said 'no-no' will be going to another camp. So I stuck with that, and so I was able to stay in camp.
JA: Did you know some people who went into the armed service?
AM: Yes, some of them had volunteered and some of 'em were drafted. There was one friend who went to the same high school with me. He was in the ROTC and he became an officer and, let's see... he was in Manzanar, too. And after he left for the armed force, I never saw him after that, but I know he didn't get killed in action or anything. Let's see, I haven't heard too many of my friends getting killed in action. Some of my friends went to South, South Pacific as an interpreter and things like that. They went to the language school.
JA: Tell me what you know about the 442nd.
AM: Well, the only thing I knew about it was they were all from Hawaii, most of them. I thought they were quite outstanding the way they fought for their country, which I, which I could never do myself, but these fellows really put their life on the line and went out there, and a lot of them got killed. But because of them, they got the most unit citation and got cited by the President of the United States, and because of that, all that good record, I think it helped the Japanese Americans even to this day.
JA: I think a lot of them did come from camps in this country, too.
AM: Yes, there were some, yes. Uh-huh. I can't think of anybody -- there probably are friends of mine that were in there, too, but I can't think of any one person right now.
AL: Can you ask him what specific question Ralph Merritt asked him during his second loyalty interview?
JA: Was there a specific question that Ralph Merritt asked you during that second interview?
AL: What he would do if the Japanese landed.
AM: One of them I remember distinctly was, he said, "Okay, if the Japanese landed in the Pacific Coast and if you were in the army, would you shoot them?" I thought and thought, you know, because I was in Japan for just three years from the time I was about eight until eleven, I guess. The place where I lived in Japan the three years was an army town, and I was kind of familiar with some of those people and I thought, "What a hard question he asked me." I thought, even though they're my enemy, I lived in that little town in Japan. I was trying to avoid answering that question, so the only thing I could come up with was, "You know, that's the only, that's the reason why I don't want to go into the army, is because I hate to shoot people." So I just kind of got around that question that way.
JA: You did a quick two-step there.
AM: [Laughs]
JA: That was a good answer. [Addressing AL] Something else in mind?
AM: How security changed over time?
JA: Oh, yeah, right. She's asking how did you observe the camp security change over time.
AM: Oh, well, the security was very strict at the beginning, as I recall, but after about one year or so, there were not as many MP I notice patrolling anymore. In fact, my father and I and my brother went to the MP camp where they were practicing in a fire drill. We took a lot of photographs there, and at the beginning I used to see the jeep carrying the soldier from one tower to another quite often, but you didn't, about a year or two, after one year, you didn't see too much of that. In fact, you know, some of the tower didn't even have any MP up there. So they were not that strict. I guess... I guess there was no attempt to escape or anything, except for going fishing. [Laughs]
JA: Did a lot of people go fishing?
AM: I think quite a few did.
JA: We talked to somebody else this week that used to ride out on a truck, actually, and go fishing. But they tasted good knowing how you caught 'em.
