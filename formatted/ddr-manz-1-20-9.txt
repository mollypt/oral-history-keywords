AL: What are your impressions about how your family dealt with going -- and we've talked a little bit about your older brother, and your sister having to leave behind her things. I'm just curious about your memories or comments that they made later about their adjustment to life at Rohwer.
TO: Well, I have to tell you that while I think that, and I talk a little bit about my growth as a result of those circumstances, but I was just thinking, I was at the cemetery on Mothers' Day to take some flowers, and I think how fortunate I am that, that my parents were very strong and as the community was just focused on how do you survive, how do you keep all the ugliness away from their children. And I don't know, they just seemed so... I mean, they had to fight deportation, my dad, my dad who always had a white collar and a tie and a hat, would go off and seek day labor jobs in order to put food on the table.
AL: Was this before the war, or afterwards?
TO: This is after the war, and there were about three families in the house, and so, one man, he took up gardening, folks took up gardening, another I think worked as a dishwasher. His family, Mr. Saito. My father would go out and he'd have his hat on, and his shirt and his tie, and then he'd go out and do whatever he could find. My mother had a little victory garden in the back yard, and she took in sewing, so we managed to, to eat. I'd like to share another story about my dad's first job: he got hired by Columbia Records, and in the olden days they pressed records and you had to be pretty strong, and most of the work force was black. And I think part of how I feel towards people of different groups, have been affected, of course, my lack of experiences, but also in terms of African Americans, there were really no African Americans in our experience up to that time. And my father said that they taught him how, they took care of him and protected him and whatever, and so he always said, "Never say bad things," and, "They are my friends." And I, I remember that. The other thing that came out of that experience is that I guess he would bring home a lot of rejects, and so there we were, this poor little family in East L.A. but we'd be listening to Tchaikovsky and Chopin, all these wonderful, wonderful symphony pieces.
AL: About what year was that? Was that prewar or postwar?
TO: Post.
AL: Postwar, okay. His first job back after the war.
TO: Yeah. But ultimately he ended up back with, in the Japanese importing, trading business.
