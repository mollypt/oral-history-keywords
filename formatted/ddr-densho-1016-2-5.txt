SE: Father wanted be like all the other Americans, and so he would take the streetcar, I don't know wherever he was going, but he said all the men were, had newspapers, they were all looking at newspapers and so he bought one. And he said he thinks he had it upside down pretending like he was reading the paper like everyone else. Because actually, he came from a very poor family. And he only went to school almost like kindergarten, just the first grade, but they had to help with the family finances, so he'd go up in the woods and collect firewood and help with the family that way. So it was quite difficult for them because he had no schooling. So I really don't know how and when he had time to learn to read Japanese, and then eventually read somewhat of the English language. He really couldn't read too much, but then he understood a lot about architecture, and so he went into real estate, and much of his was planning. He's supposed to have built a lot of the buildings in Seattle, made the drawings, and he... the ES Hotel where we lived on Maynard Avenue, he had originally made the drawings, then they built the hotel, yes.
AD: Seiko, how was your childhood different than, say, Pat's? What was it like for you to grow up as a Japanese American? What was different for you than, say, your kids?
SE: Well, I don't know that it'd been that much different. What?
Off camera: You spoke Japanese.
SE: Yeah, well, we spoke Japanese, so it was difficult when you went to school. But, see, I was number six in the family of eight children, so my older brothers and sisters were already in school. They had a more difficult time than I because they started to speak little bit of English at home and so I would pick up some things. But as a whole, we spoke Japanese.
AD: Do you remember anything, anything that was difficult about being Japanese American? Do you remember being... this is before the war. Do you remember being treated differently or not? Did you feel like you were a part of...
SE: Well, we lived in an area where most of the neighbors were Japanese. There were a few Caucasian and a few Chinese. In fact, two doors away there was a family of Chinese and we were very good friends. We played together, stayed overnight at each others' homes, and I don't know. It seems like we were all out there playing together. But as I say, most of them were Japanese. There were big apartment houses, and so one of the big apartment houses, there were different Caucasians. But I don't think there were any blacks, but there were Chinese and Japanese living close by. In fact, one of the girls that helped the church here was in my kindergarten class and she was about a half a block away.

AD: Did you think of yourself as an American? How did you think of yourself?
SE: Well, being in a school that everything was in English, and we were taught from the beginning the "Pledge of Allegiance," and we had a very understanding principal, and she watched over us like a mother hen. But every morning when we went to school there was a... on the main floor of the school there was two court, and one was for the boys and one was for the girls, but every morning we would all gather in that area, and each class had their own place to stand. I think you stood in groups of, I don't know if it was two or three of us, and we would all stand and gather there when the school bell rang. And the principal would come down and speak to everyone in the morning, and then let us all march into class. So it was almost military like, we marched into our classes. But it was very orderly, and she treated us very well. And then she got so that she knew most of the children and their families. In the school there were about nine hundred students. Of them, about six hundred were Japanese and three hundred was divided among Chinese, blacks, and a few Caucasians. But she gave us a lot of the instructions in the morning when we assembled there, and then marched ordered into our classes. At the beginning, we only had one classroom each child, I mean, we all went to one class and the teacher taught us everything. But about the time I was in the third or fourth grade, they added rooms to the building and they had what they called the platoon system where we had a homeroom, and the teacher conducted all our necessary things for the day, and then we had our different rooms for... well, she had mostly the language and the English and the spelling. Then for music and for art, we had music and art and science classes, and so we would march to our other classes. So that was quite modern for that time, which was unheard of in the other areas, 'cause most of the students went to one classroom and that was it all day. One teacher teaching all the subjects. This was a very advanced school, and so we were fortunate to be a part of that.
