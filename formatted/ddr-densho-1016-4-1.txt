AD: Tell us about how you responded to the draft. Tell us how did, when that began. How did you, what was your reaction?
GA: Let me see. They had activated the draft early in January of 1944, and they put all of us -- by then, I was of draft age, just turned eighteen -- and so we were all put through the physical exam. The entire camp, draft-age fellows, were put through a physical, and were all classified physically fit to serve and given a 1-A classification. 1-A, 4-C, an "enemy alien," to a 1-A classification without due process of law. And therefore, I thought, "Gee, this is odd. This not right." This is where I started to feel that I'm not gonna listen to these, the government, because they're just pushing me around too much. So at the time that my, my induction letter came, that was two months later. By end of March, I received my induction paper, and I gave it a lot of thoughts, and I spent many a nights thinking, "What should I do? Should I go, should I not?" If I go, that means that I'll do anything that the government tells me. No, I don't want to do that. I'm just gonna say, "No, I'm not gonna be pushed around anymore." And here again, you come up with the question 27 and 28, the question 27 is where would I serve in the armed forces and go wherever ordered to go, and I said, "No, I'm not gonna do that. I'm through being pushed around." And the second one was, in short, would I pledge allegiance to the United States and forsake any pledges to the emperor or any enemy country. Naturally, as I stated before, that the United States was my country, therefore if the forty-eight states was attacked, I would go serve the, serve in the army. But at this point, I'd say "yes" to question 28. And that, that is what I mulled over, and about my dad being taken away. He was taken away for two years. Our family was put on what we referred to as a stop order, that is to keep us from going out of the camp to relocate anywhere, for some reason, I don't know. Maybe it was because my dad was being interned, but we couldn't leave camp. And numerous other things that I kept thinking, and I spent many a nights the whole month before, before the induction date. And my parents didn't know what I was thinking, I wouldn't talk about it, neither did my brother. But on the draft day came and I did not report, there they realized that, "Oh, you didn't go. Well, this is your decision you made, it's all up to you, because this is your future." But prior to that, my parents told, told me that, "This is your decision, and if you should go one way or the other, go with your head held high. Don't turn back; be proud of what you're doing."
[Interruption]
GA: Let me go on with my story here. 'Cause one of the important thing was my parents told me to go with head held high. When they found out that I refused to go, then my mother says, "Give me a clipping of your hair and your fingernail and I'll put it in an envelope. So that if anything should happen to you, that we may never see you again, at least we can have services for you with the contents of this envelope." And she put my name down and the date. And that was right before the FBI, or the marshal came and showed his badge and says, "You're under arrest." So gathered up a few belongings, and they escorted, he escorted me to a car sitting outside on the, the road. And when I got there, I looked and there was another marshal sitting in there, but in the back seat there was another fellow. I looked, and oh my gosh, that was somebody I knew. And so I was real relieved that I wasn't the only one. That's the first time I found out that there was anybody else that was not going. So anyway, we wound up going to the gate, and the marshals told the sentry, "Mark them off for indefinite leave. We don't know what's gonna happen to 'em." So we started our way up to the Boise county jail, and on the way over, the marshals tried to talk us out of resisting. They told us that, "Boy, you guys are missing, you did a, you're doing the wrong thing. Just see what you're losing? See all the freedom, the, all the nice things?" He forgot that he had just taken us out of an internment camp, and we're taking, being taken to a jail this time. Now, what do you think of that? Naturally we were scared, but also I was disgusted with the marshal for bringing up subjects such as that.
In the evening, we got over the county jail and they fingerprinted us, and they put us in our cells, we were in a, what referred to as a tank where there were others that were there, but we were assigned cells and we went in there. That night I went to sleep, in the morning, got up feeling itchy all over. And pull up my shirt and found little red spot all over my body. I looked, and there are bites all along the waistline and all over. So just wondering, "What in the heck is this?" Lifted up the mattress, and the mattress was full of bedbugs. Never seen a bedbugs before, but you know, they look like watermelon seeds. And when they get fat, they get plump. They're flat, but then when they take your blood they just get fat, so they look like watermelon seed. And it took us days and days to get those, get rid of those bedbugs, yeah. That's one of the sad experiences. [Laughs]

But the main penitentiary, I went in there, I had a sentence of three years, three months, and spent the majority of my time in the main institution, and about a half a year over in the prison farm where it was, you were more, given more liberty, yeah.
