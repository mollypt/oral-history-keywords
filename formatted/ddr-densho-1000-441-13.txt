TI: So you've been away from your parents for a year.
LH: My father had passed away.
TI: Oh, during that time?
LH: He passed away in 1945.
TI: Okay, so when you were in Des Moines, he passed away?
LH: Uh-huh.
TI: Did he pass away in Des Moines, or was he...
LH: In Des Moines.
TI: What happened?
LH: He had a heart attack on Christmas Eve, he died at Christmas Eve. So Christmas Eves were hard.
TI: And was he ill or anything?
LH: No.
TI: So this was a shock for...
LH: We don't know what happened, he just had trouble breathing, you know, shortness of breath, and fortunately the hospital was next door. And he went into a coma, so he never came out of it.
TI: Because he was a fairly young man, he was in his fifties.
LH: But Tom later said he was working in a press shop, and the work was too heavy for him. And he did complain about the work a little bit. But being anIsseiguy, a man, you stick it out.
TI: That must have been devastating for your mother.
LH: It was, totally, because he was the bread earner. Yes, it was very hard on her.
TI: And so at this point, it sounds like George started a business in Seattle, so your mother went to go live with him?
LH: My father died in December, and so probably in June or so, my mother went to Seattle (to help him with his business).
TI: That makes sense. I can see why your mom would want you there, too.
LH: Yeah. And the reason I was going to work a year was my brothers felt I was really too immature to go to college, and they were right. I was really a very immature kid. Plus, I didn't have any money, a partial scholarship wasn't going to be enough. So I needed to save money, that's why. And then I received another letter from Simpson where the gentleman said, "Well, we don't have assignments yet for part time jobs," so apparently I applied for a part time job.
TI: So when you went back to Seattle, did you go to the University of Washington?
LH: Yes.
TI: And what kind of courses did you take?
LH: Oh, not surprising, I decided I would like to be a social worker because of Ross Wilbur and what he was doing for the Japanese Americans. And I was taking some soc. classes, but I thought, oh, this wasn't at all what I thought sociology would be. [Laughs] So I went into education; I knew I'd have to get a job.
TI: And so you became a teacher.
LH: Right.
TI: And what level, what grade level did you teach?
LH: I started intermediate.
TI: So that'd be like...
LH: Fourth grade.
TI: Fourth grade.
LH: Uh-huh, fourth grade is where I started. It was a good place to start.
TI: And how long a career did you have in teaching?
LH: Well, I taught full time until about 1960, and then I retired for a while.
TI: And that's when you had your children?
LH: Because I wanted to have a child. And so our first, David, was born in '66. Then once I had David, I did not teach until Mari came along and she was in the fourth grade, I went part time back to teaching. And once she was in high school, I went full time again. So it's that kind of thing. I taught twenty-five years.
