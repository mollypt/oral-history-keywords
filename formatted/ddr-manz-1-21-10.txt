AL: How would you describe your family's economic situation compared to your, your neighborhood? Were you considered a well-off family, a poor family, rich?
YK: We were all poor. Yes, well, for one thing, my oldest brother used to deliver newspaper,Rafu Shimpo. I did that, too, later, but so he brought in thirty dollars a month, I think. And my sister, by then she was in high school, going to... what... oh, forget what, she was helping someone at the... what was it? I can't remember what they were doing.
AL: Is this Chiyoko?
YK: Yes. So she had pitched in. Oh, she was working for a nurse, I think. And I, I hadn't gotten out to work yet, but...
AL: You said she worked for a nurse?
YK: Yes.
AL: A nurse, okay. What was your first job?
YK: My first job was working delivering the newspaper.
AL: TheRafu?
YK: Uh-huh.
AL: How much did you make?
YK: Thirty-five dollars a month, which was not bad.
AL: That's twice as much as you made in camp. [Laughs]
YK: Yeah, I know. Yes. I had the East L.A. route, and newspaper was all bicycle, riding from First and Los Angeles where the publishing company was, going up the First Street Bridge, which was steep. That time, it's not graded like it is now. Then the flats on the riverside, east side of the river, then another grade, which was longer still, than the bridge.
AL: How far did you go?
YK: Well, from the hill, we went to Evergreen Cemetery, that's quite a few blocks, besides going in and out over First Street, because First Street was the main drag, and people had stores on First Street, barber shop, grocery shop, regular residences, churches, Buddhist churches, and they were scattered north of First and south of First Street as far as Fourth Street and as far as Cesar Chavez, well, twenty-four blocks north. So that was seven days a week, Sunday was a time for [inaudible] which I had to borrow my father's pick-up, panel, little panel car, the load got so heavy.
AL: Did you deliver in the morning or at night?
YK: Morning, morning. But daily was afternoon. And Sunday was nice because the kids, younger kids, would like to help for a hamburger. [Laughs]
AL: So you were running your own business?
YK: Yeah, well, yeah.
AL: Do you know what kind of a truck your dad, the year and the model?
YK: He had a Rio, I don't think they make them anymore, and a gear shift. And the other pickup was a little Ford.
AL: Were these, like, 1920s, 1930s?
YK: Oh, yes. And good thing we had a big garage, and I think it could hold the truck and the pickup.
AL: Did your family own a house, or did they rent?
YK: Rental. Most houses were rentals.
AL: Did you rent from a Japanese American?
YK: No, no. That time, even... well, I guess there weren't too many olderNiseis. I don't know, some of them are older, but they didn't know the way to get around to get younger son to become property owner, which was illegal, but I guess they didn't know the ropes. Because my landlord, our landlord was a French, I guess he was a second-generation, and when he came to collect the rent, he spoke English, signed his name very, very well in English.
[Interruption]
AL: So he was a FrenchNisei?
YK: Yeah. Because we had, oh, few French -- Los Files Street, Los Files is named for the French family. I thought it was strange, but...
AL: I thought so, too.
YK: Yes. But when you, when you say Los Files in Spanish, you're supposed to say plural. And, but this is a family, Los Files, so it's French. I learned that much later.
AL: That's interesting.
