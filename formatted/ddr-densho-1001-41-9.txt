JD: Could you say something about the most dangerous things that you remember that you had to do when you were in Burma?
GH: Well, I had a close call. This was during the battle of Shaduzup. The 1st Battalion to which I was assigned was ordered to place a road block. The 1st Battalion -- each battalion is divided into two columns or combat teams and we were designated a color -- the White Combat Team, during the evening, they crossed the Irrawaddy River, and the Red Combat Team, to which I was assigned, was... served as cover. That morning I received a call to cross the river in daylight and to assist in the tapping into the enemy telephone line. When an interpreter makes a move you usually have an escort. On this occasion I had two escorts, one in front and one in back. And the water was shoulder deep so I had my carbine over my head. I also carried a pouch with contained two dictionaries and a map, and that had to be lifted to keep it dry, but also obstructing my view. When we arrived in the middle of the stream, I heard three shots. It went "Chung, chung, chung." The sniper was aiming at us. That's all I remember. Fortunately we all crossed without being hit. When I crossed the river I reported to the officer in charge and he said that the telephone line was dead. So I was not able to perform my duties, but my crossing was not in vain because when the White Combat Team struck, the Japanese left with their breakfast behind. So, I was able to enjoy rice and a can of sardines and that was quite a feast for me. Because I allergic to k-rations and I was in pretty bad shape because I would break out in hives when I ate the canned goods. I was allergic to the preservative. So I had to exchange my canned goods with crackers and chocolate, and it was very difficult to survive on that and march all day.
It was, as I recall, the battle of Shaduzup was very successful. We were waiting for air drop and day's rest. That's when we heard that the 2nd Battalion, to which Roy was assigned, were under siege. That we were forced to... we were placed on forced march, which meant that we would march day and night to rescue the 2nd Battalion. Well, the battalion medical officer was aware of my plight. During the ten minute break he called me and he said, "Grant, I know that you're suffering, but if you reach the point that you can no longer keep up with the column..." he said, "we'll leave you with the friendly natives, the Kachin. We will place them with buckets of silver rupee." When I asked, "If I survive, how do I get back?" He said, "You're on your own." I said, "Thanks, but no thanks." Then he authorized me to place my pack on a horse. But when the horse suffered a shrapnel wound it had to be destroyed. Then my pack was put on a mule. But the mule, while climbing a steep incline, the load shifted and it made a terrible scar. It too had to be destroyed. The second mule that was carrying my pack collapsed during the ten minute break, and the mule skinner kept pulling and pulling and mule wouldn't budge. When the ten minute break was up and the last men disappeared around the bend, my heart sank. Because I said to unload the mule to get my pack and then do double time to catch up with the --

JD: ...the mule collapsed and you realized you were going to have to grab your own bags and run to catch up.
GH: So the pack was placed on the second mule and the mule, while climbing a steep incline, the load shifted and it caused a mean sore and it too had to be destroyed. My pack was now put on the second mule and during the ten minute break the mule collapsed. And the mule skinner kept on pulling, pulling, but it wouldn't budge. Finally when I saw the last man disappear around the bend, my heart sank because I knew it would be very difficult for me to unpack the mule, carry the pack, and double time to catch up. Just then the mule skinner kept pulling, pulling, and finally he cursed. He went through all the vocabulary of curse words. Then he made a desperate pull and he shouted, "Damn it! You volunteered too!" And the mule got up. [Laughs] Fortunately I was able to hang on to the mule's tail and continue with the trip. I still remember the mule looking back from time to time as though to say, "Hey, give me a break." But we did finally make it to Nhpum Ga, where Roy's battalion was under siege.

JD: What happened when you reached Nhpum Ga?
GH: At Nhpum Ga... well, they were under siege for, I think it was thirteen days. The battle was such that it became known as the "Maggot Hill." It was in the jungle with the enemy's bodies, mules, that couldn't be buried. Roy, who was one of the last persons still active, he crawled from time to time. One night he crawled beyond the perimeter into no man's land and he overheard that the enemy was to strike at dawn. With that information he went back and he reported to Lieutenant McLogan the platoon leader. He in turn withdrew his men from the bridge and they, I think booby trapped the foxhole so after they withdrew and established a new perimeter. They concentrated all the heavy automatic weapons in the direction of the attack and then waited, and at dawn they attacked. The enemy was taken by surprise to find the position vacated. Then they charged up the hill and Lieutenant McLogan waited 'til they were within fifteen yards. Then they opened up with all of these automatic weapons and they annihilated the enemy.
Well, the second wave that was coming up, they saw what happened. They had to go around. And Roy's thinking that they maybe we'll draw out the fight another day. He rose from his foxhole and gave a command in Japanese, "Charge! Charge!" And charge they did, only to face the same fate as the first wave. And when the battle was over, they counted fifty-two bodies, including two officers, and with that the siege was over. It happened to be on Sunday, Easter Sunday. Thanks to Roy, I think the battalion was saved.
