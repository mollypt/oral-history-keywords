AL: Today   is Saturday, May 18, 2002, and this is a continuation of the   Manzanar Oral History interview with Rose Ochi. Today we're at the   New Otani Hotel in Los Angeles. And Rose, last night, when we   ended, we were talking about, your involvement with Min Yasui. And   I'd like to step back and ask you to talk about how you got   involved with JACL, and when.
TO: Okay.   I never was active at the local chapter level or at the regional   level, other than I would be invited to speak at a gathering, or   participate in some activity at the local chapter level. At the   time, I was working in the mayor's office, and it's a   seven-days-a-week job, and I was also involved with a lot of civil   rights and professional organizations. And so JACL structure was   not something that... I don't know how to say this... they had a   history often of being more involved in some power struggles and   all, and I'm someone very goal-oriented and interested in spending   my energies and focus on getting things done. And so I just didn't   have, I guess, what I would say, the patience to be involved in   some of that activity. But having served on the Select Commission   on Immigration and Refugee Policy, Floyd Shimomura, young   president, one of the probably the youngest presidents at JACL,   invited --
AL: How do   you spell his last name?
TO:   S-H-I-M-O-M-U-R-A. He invited me to -- actually, they just   appointed me, the board voted for me at the national level, to make   me vice president, because they wanted to bring my contacts and   know-how at working at the national level, having spent years doing   that for the city as an advocate on policy, and now, with intimate   contacts, with personal contacts with many of the senators and   House members of the Judiciary, they thought that I could be very   helpful. I mentioned last night about just how I got much of the   staff from the Select Commission hired by the new Wartime   Commission, but most importantly, the hiring of Aiko Herzig, who   uncovered the information about the lack of military necessity in   rounding up American of Japanese... during World War II. I, so I   was selected, and invited to come to play a special role.
AL: So   this would be during, like, the late '70s or the early '80s?
TO: It   would be the beginning of the Reagan Administration.
AL: And   how long were you vice president?
TO:   Probably for about four years. And we won't talk about the run for   president. [Laughs]
AL:   Actually, I would love to hear about it.
TO: But   actually, I did, I ran for president and lost by two votes. I   understand there were a lot of proxy revocations occurring during   the night when they did a straw poll, and discovered that I may win   and be the first woman, young president, I was considered young.   It's a nice thing about JACL is even then, I was considered young,   because, like in a lot of organizations, you know, you get an old   guard, and they want to retain power, and anyone who's not a part   of that team is threatening. And organizations go through those   kinds of interesting kinds of struggles. But in any event, I wanted   to continue to be involved, although I was no longer in an official   capacity, and so I did work with the community and on my own, to   seek to promote the redress bill. And there were a number of   meetings that were very significant. One meeting with Congressmen   Rodino, he said he wanted to see me, and I was actually there   meeting with him...
AL: Who   was he the congressman for?
TO: He   was, he's from New Jersey, but he was head of the Judiciary   Committee, so he was a powerful man. And he wanted to know why --   and I don't want this to sound like a slam on our Japanese American   members -- but he said, "Why aren't they pushing the bill?" And I   told him that it's difficult for them because they do, they don't   represent all Japanese American districts, and there could be   repercussions for them. In fact, I believe Norm's house had some   racial slurs, graffiti...
AL: This   is Norman Mineta?
TO:   Mineta, congressman at the time from San Jose. So I think it would   be great if you could have someone else take the lead on it. And he   moved on that, and appointed Barney Frank from Massachusetts, who's   a very liberal congressman who embraced the idea and who would have   no fallout from his constituents. Other things, I'd like to tell   you a little bit about the story of Senator Alan Simpson, because I   think it's a very, very significant... Alan and I became very good   friends on the Select Commission because at the time, the Democrats   were in control, when we were first constituted. And he and   Hamilton Fish on the House side were the lowest folks on the totem   pole, 'cause it was a Democratic administration, and Democrats   Rodino and Kennedy, who were heads of the Judiciary, so they, they   were in power. And so he was at the low end of the totem pole, and   so were the two public members, so I'd either sit with Hamilton   Fish or Alan Simpson. And while we didn't agree on all issues,   immigration is kind of an issue where you can be switching sides,   you know, a very conservative person can align with a liberal   person maybe about national ID cards. So it's kind of interesting   bedfellows as we moved forward and all, and we became very close.   One time we were on a trip; we held national hearings around the   country. And we had dinner, and he told me the story that as a   young boy scout, living in Cody, Wyoming, that he would, with his   troop, go across barbed-wire fences to have joint meetings with the   Japanese American boy scouts that were interned there in Wyoming.   And he said that one boy scout and he got along famously, and they   became pen pals, and they wrote to each other through high school   and college, and then somehow lost touch. Alan went to law school,   and then held elective office, and Norm Mineta became the mayor of   San Jose and then went on to Congress. And one day Alan saw his   name, contacted him, and then when they both ended up in D.C., they   renewed their friendship.
AL: So   Norm Mineta was the boy scout?
TO: Norm   Mineta was the boy scout. As we moved forward on the redress bill,   I... their relationship was very important. I went to see Alan, and   Alan was then the minority whip. And I believe but for his, his   pushing on behalf of this bill, it would have never happened. He   was someone that was very conservative, and yet that's no enemy to   the redress, because as a conservative, he believed in the   Constitution, and what it meant for all Americans. And so I used to   go see him, I have this wonderful picture where I... well, I have   to go back a little bit, this is a little disjointed. I went to see   him earlier, when we were on the Commission together, and I, when I   was waiting in his conference room, I saw these beautiful paintings   of the Grand Tetons, so when he came to join me, I told him, "Oh,   this is Spencer's Mountain..." and he said, "How would a girl from   L.A. know about that?" And I says, "Well, I says, "my husband's   uncle, Uncle Fred, is a watercolor painter, and he gave us a very   similar painting for our wedding gift." And he said, "Not Fred   Ochi, the famous red barn painter?" And I says, "Yes," and so   anyway, I gave him, from Uncle Fred, a red barn painting, I have a   picture of me giving it to him, and this is after the redress bill   passed. But he was very instrumental, because he was close to the   White House, and he was able to round up the votes. And I don't   want to suggest that that was pivotal, but you know, 'cause it was   a massive effort from all sectors of our community at that point,   and the veterans were very, very key. And there are many, many   stories that you can get from all the organizations that were   involved.
