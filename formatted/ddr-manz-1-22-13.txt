AL: What do you remember of the train ride?
YK: On a train, yes. Although we used to hitchhike on freight cars in the summer, going to Fresno, pick grapes, fruits. And what a ride. The steel cars would be so hot from the sun in the daytime, kept us nice and warm on the ride up to pick.
AL: So you went by train to the station in Lone Pine, right?
YK: Yes. And typical desert dust storm [inaudible].
AL: How did you get to Manzanar?
YK: Well, the buses.
AL: How do you describe a desert dust storm?
YK: Well, you had to close your eyes when a gust came around, and you gotta clear your ears of dust and wipe off your mouth and all that, 'cause coated on your lips. And sometimes you get stung by little gravels, your ears would sting with the little gravel, pinging in eardrum, ears. So it was something new, it was something new. Well, we got used to it.
AL: What did you do that first day? I mean...
YK: Well, we had to, many of the families had to stuff a canvas bag full of straw for the mattress to sleep on. And the cots were steel cots, some were the canvas bedding type. We were just married week before, we were put in with a family of five.
AL: Did you know them?
YK: No.
AL: Who were they?
YK: Well, later, later I heard that they were, they owned a dry... a dry cleaners. They owned a dry cleaners on San Pedro Street, and the oldest one was a girl, she was probably junior high school age, the second was, oh, was probably a sixth grader, and the youngest was third, fourth grade, and two, the father and mother. And so naturally, next morning, went to housing to see what could be done. They changed our, our beds to single bachelor. I knew these fellows, they were friends of my older brother, so it wasn't bad. But that's the kind of housing some people had to endure.
