AL: So, do you, do you have any memories of the war, in the sense of Pearl Harbor? Any recollection of that happening or your life changing, when the war started?
TO: Very honestly, I can't say that I remember. All I do feel is, you know, you have a sense of a lot of fears, a lot of, I mentioned earlier, anger, and a lot of sadness. And so I think, even if you don't understand, and you can't actually remember, you have absorbed a lot of those feelings. And that I find that, even today, that they somehow find a way to surface. Recently I went to a dedication at Santa Anita racetrack, where our family was interned for about six months, living in stables before we were assigned to Rohwer. And then that morning that we went there, I... this was this spring. I was on a tram moving about the stable area, and I will tell you, I was just, tears were just pouring out of my eyes. And I didn't know whether it was from the hay fever, or just a lot of subliminal pain. I, this, everybody, we had lunch after that, and there was plaque dedication and it was a very happy occasion to see a lot of the other internees, and, but then they were going to do a day at the races, and stay on, and I told my husband, "I want to go home." And I found that I was totally exhausted, and I think it's because of some of the, these feelings that somehow surfaced.
I can tell you another experience, and it involved... as you know, we have the annual Manzanar pilgrimage. And in the old days, we had a big potluck, rather than people doing on their own. We put the tables out and everybody put the food down. And because I was up there a couple of days earlier, and couldn't cook, I decided to go into town and buy some fruits and cookies to share. And one of the cookies I bought was ginger snaps. And I don't particularly like ginger snaps, but somehow I reached out and got a box of ginger snaps. And I ate one, and I was just overcome with nausea. And I just felt terrible, and I didn't know why. And it occurred to me, that when we were on this long train ride to Rohwer, they would give us sandwiches and ginger snaps. There are connections.
AL: When, when your family was relocated to Santa Anita, were you in San Francisco at the time, or down here?
TO: We had moved to Los Angeles. I was born in Los Angeles, as was my brother.
AL: Okay. So do you know what your address was in Los Angeles? I mean, were you in the Japanese community?
TO: I was in what is called Boyle Heights, and I was born at the Japanese American Hospital, and I lived on Dakota Street, I don't know the exact number, but I can get that information.
AL: Okay. Do you remember the train ride? Or do you remember -- I guess I should step back, do you remember Santa Anita, actually being there?
TO: I only have, like, memories, part of it is the smell. I think they just white-washed the stables, and you could smell them, and the hay. And so I have hay fever to this day, but I think that I remember those smells. The only thing that I remember was one time peeking out of the fence and watching some, what I thought were rich people having a barbecue, and I remember that. And the train ride, I don't remember places, but I do remember this long journey. Just sort of everything else that was passing, it was a blur. It's possible they might have had shades drawn.
