JD: Just, so to backtrack just a little bit. Once you... you've been selected for the 5307, you knew you were going to Burma, what did you know about what your mission was there?
GH: Actually, we didn't know 'til... I did hear about it during the training in India.
JD: What did they tell you?
GH: Well, as I recall, it was the long range penetration behind enemy line, and that we were to disrupt enemy communication, enemy supply lines, and the strongholds.
Off camera: Why was that important to the war? Why was that important to the war?
GH: When the war broke out, President Roosevelt was very much aware that it was important to keep China in the war and thereby tying down the Japanese forces in China that would otherwise be sent to the South Pacific against our troops. But in order to keep China in the war, we had to supply them with the necessary war supplies, ammunition. But China, being under, in war with Japan for ten years, the coastline was occupied by Japan. When the Japanese occupied Burma, the main land line, which was the Burma Road, was also cut off. So in desperation the Americans did use the C-46 and C-47 transport planes to supply the Chinese over the Himalaya Mountains. We called it the "humps." But that was very limited. We had to get that line of, land line, open. But in order to open the land line, which the plan called for, from Ledo -- which is at the north east corner of India -- from Ledo...
[Interruption]
GH: Our purpose was to open the old Burma Road. In order to do that, we wanted to connect the road also known as... the Ledo Road, also known as Togall Road, from Ledo, which is in northern Assam down to Myitkyina and then to tie up with the old Burma Road. So we had to get the Japanese out of north Burma. And our mission was to capture the all weather airstrip that was in Myitkyina. Which we succeeded after five major battles and thirty some odd skirmish.
Off camera: Can you describe the conditions of what it was like? What it was like?
GH: In the jungle? This was very difficult, going through jungle. Actually there were 3,000 men that volunteered. Our ground transportation consisted of seven hundred mules and horses. And our supplies were all sent by air drop, by parachute. Each parachute drop would contain five days' rations, k-rations for each individual, medical supplies, ammunition, and fodder for the mules. Now and then we did receive a ten and one ration, which is much more elaborate. But I understand that the k-rations were for emergency purposes, not for daily rations. But, there we were. I think people were having malnutrition because they lacked the proper nutrition.
The jungle weather was unfavorable. You had so many diseases. Malaria was quite common. Dysentery was quite common. It was very difficult. Even, you couldn't smoke at night because... for smokers it was very difficult because you couldn't smoke at night. Because otherwise they had what we call, Washing Machine Charlie, that's the Japanese zeroes flying at night, and if we were to light they could detect the light. The weather was very unfavorable. Climbing mountains during the rain, it's very slippery. It was quite common to see the mules with these large overloads just fall over. There were many people who suffered, but we didn't leave any wounded soldiers. What they did was... originally they had a liaison plane bringing in messages and someone got the idea, "Why don't you take someone back?" As a matter of fact I was evacuated once by plane. We called it a puddle jumper. It was a small plane, and I might just explain how it took off. The plane landed in the rice field. When I got to the rice field, they were tying the plane to a tree with the spare parachute ropes. I was too tired to ask why. I got on. The pilot did say that we will have to wait 'til sundown because we were so behind enemy lines. At sundown, the pilot got in the cockpit and started the motor. He had to get the rpms going, so the plane is jumping up and down. I think when the rpm, it reached the right rpm, I think the machete went down and the plane took off. On very uneven footing, it was shaking and I said... I could see the bamboo right in front of me, I said, oh my god. But the plane did take off. I could see the bamboo swaying. We just barely made it. And we meandered and we made it to Ledo safely.
