gky: After -- can you tell me a little about Frank Hachiya?
GH: Yes. Frank was my classmate. He was also my bunkmate. We were very close. I first heard of his demise when I was flying to Chunking to Kunming when I was ordered to interrogate an officer. While I was on the plane, I saw aTimemagazine. I picked it up and I read it, and there was an article on Frank Hachiya, and that is how I learned that he was killed in Leyte. Frank was a scholarly-type person, very philosophical, very strong in Japanese. Frank actually was with headquarters and he need not volunteer, but when they asked for a volunteer he made a jump behind enemy lines to map out the Japanese position prior to the invasion of Leyte. I think it was about two weeks before the landing. He was able to complete the mapping of the enemy position, and as he was returning with that information, he had to cross in front of the Japanese line. There is two versions. One says that he was shot by friendly fire, and the other he was shot by a sniper. But he was mortally wounded and he was dying, but he crawled and he crawled and made it to the line and was able to display the map in front of the officer, and shortly thereafter he died.
gky: It's hard for you to repeat that.
GH: Yeah. Very difficult.
gky: Well, another thing that was very difficult about him was he was dishonored by the American Legion.
GH: Yes. I think it was about a month, was it, before he was killed. The American Legion Post in Oregon, they removed his name from the County Clerk Memorial, along with fifteen others. I think sixteen names of Japanese descent was crossed out. Frank, for his valor, did receive the Distinguished Service Cross, perhaps the highest decoration received by aNiseiin the Pacific war.
gky: And then what happened with -- did they ever get their names reinstated?
GH: Yes, they reinstated their names. The pressure was so strong that they did restore the names.
