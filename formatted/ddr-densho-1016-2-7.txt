AD: And then, Seiko, you guys had to move from Seattle. What were the circumstances?
SE: Well, there were just rumors flying at that time, because we had no way of knowing. And let's see. There were all kinds of thoughts that we were going to be put behind bars, and it was a scary time. I'll go to the story about that night. One night, in the middle of the night, I felt somebody pulling my covers off, and somebody was leaning over me. I woke up without realizing anything, I screamed, evidently, 'cause I hurt myself. I think I woke up with my own scream, I don't know. And then I sat up and I saw this figure leaving. My room was on the second floor of the house, and there was a porch, back porch, on the bottom, and then ours was another porch on top. And there was this thing... all of a sudden I can't think of the -- retaining post that held up the porch. I don't think there was a roof over that porch, but that I can't remember. But when this fellow took off out of the door that he had come in and slid down the post, my scream had woken the family, so I went after him wondering which way he went and who it was and all. And he slid down the post and then ran around the house and I could hear the footsteps echoing as he took off towards the street, towards the front of the house. So I walked back to the room thinking that the family was behind me, and when I heard my father snoring, I don't know. I barely was able to walk down the stairs 'cause I was shaking so badly. And I shook Dad and woke him up and I told him what happened. And he went out to look to see where he had... and there was a vine covering that post, and it was all broken, and he had shimmied up that post. And so I said, "Dad, we got to call the police." And Dad says, "No use," he said. He said a lot of the -- he had never told us these things, but he said, "Lot of the Japanese homes have been broken into, things have happened, but," he said, "the police don't even bother to come. So he said, "No use calling." And so I don't know whether I went back to sleep in my bed or not, but it made it very uncomfortable to be there in that house anymore. But the next day, my brother, who was living downtown, came up and fixed the bolt on that door so that it would lock good. Because being upstairs, we never thought that it needed a good lock, and maybe I didn't even bother to bolt it.

AD: How do you feel about the way that Japanese Americans were treated after Pearl Harbor?
SE: At the time, I didn't think that there was... we were being treated any different than it might have been if it was... well, I suppose because it was war with Japan, we felt that it was, kind of some of the things that might happen. But at first, I left... let's see, I left Seattle end of February I think it was. First of March, the Christian Youth Council that I belonged to always has a conference, one year in Seattle, one year in Spokane, and another year in Yakima. So this was the year that was supposed to be Spokane, and since they announced at that, at one of the first times that they talked about moving us from the coast, they said that the Seattle area was Area 1, and east of the mountains was Area 2, mid, and then Spokane was number 3 I think it was. And 3 was an area that they didn't consider evacuating, and the 2 was possibility, but 1 was definitely going to be moved. And so I told Dad, I said, "You know, I'm a citizen of this country, and I don't feel that I could stand being put behind barbed wire." I said, "When I come out, I would feel very resentful to my country and I don't want to feel bitter against my own country." So I said, "As long as I'm going to Spokane," I said, "I'd like to go and find some kind of a housework position where I have a home to stay in." And there was no thought of inviting my folks or anything, I just wanted to get out. So he said okay, but I never knew whether I could find lodging or not. And when I got to Spokane, I asked the cab driver to take me to a nice Japanese hotel, and he took me to, he just drove around downtown and then took me to this skid row hotel. And the man that was at the desk didn't know who owned the hotel, and their name or anything. So then I went down to Sides corner which is on the corner of Trent and Stevens, and asked them if there was other Japanese-owned hotels. And then I was able to go to this hotel, and, you know, they have those hotels in Trent Alley and went up to the J-A Hotel, and there inside the building I saw a girl that I knew in Seattle. And so I told her I had some friends in Spokane, so she called them immediately and the boys came down and got me in.
AD: Did you feel lonely when you first moved here?
SE: Oh, yes, yes. It was very difficult. 'Cause it felt... well, you know, even if I knew these people, they weren't real close friends. And, you know, you feel like, out in the ocean with nothing. Almost felt like that. But Mrs. Miyake was such a kind person. When she found out that I had a room in the hotel, she says, "No way are you gonna stay in a hotel." She says, "You come and live with us." And she had four daughters and a son. She says, "You're about the same age, you almost look like my daughters, and really don't know the difference." And I had just met the oldest daughter through our Christian conference. The Japanese used, had these, what we called YPCC, and after Thanksgiving, the Friday and Saturday after Thanksgiving, they came to Spokane, I mean, Seattle, and we had the big... I can't remember. It was, anyway, Northwest YPCC. And in the wintertime, my father's hotel business is very slow and there were lots of vacant rooms, so he would open ten, twelve rooms, and let the young people come and stay. And we were required to serve breakfast for them, overnight stay and breakfast. And it was usually for Friday and Saturday nights and then they went home on Sunday after morning service. So there was this club room thatKibeis, they're second generation Japanese that were sent to Japan to be educated, and they'd come back after they... a lot of them didn't finish high school, but they'd finish high school and they'd come back, and they are kind of misfits. They don't fit in with... even in their family they had troubles because they might have younger brothers and sisters that never knew them, and they come. And so they had their own clubhouse, and my father rented them a room. It was a fairly large room where we would have two ping pong, one ping pong tables and a pool table. No, two ping pong tables, that's right. And so they would come and spend time playing in the room. So we had that room, so we would get a clean sheet over the table as tablecloth, and Dad would cook breakfast for them. And so they all loved to stay at the hotel because they had little more freedom than in a home.
