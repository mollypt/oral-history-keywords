AD: Seiko, where were you when Pearl Harbor happened? Do you remember?
SE: Pearl Harbor? We had a home up on Beacon Hill in Seattle. I had a brother that contracted tuberculosis when he was in his, finishing his junior year at University of Washington. And we had him, we were living at that time in the hotel. We all had our rooms. But we took care of him at the hotel, and he was getting worse. But we were close to, the hotel was close to city hall where we had to take him for his tests and whatever they did for him. I'm surprised they let us keep him at home, but there was no room. But then we knew he was getting worse and we were having problems with him, so Dad was looking for a place to somehow put him in a sanitarium. There was Firland Sanitarium north of Seattle, and then Steilacoom in Tacoma. But we had troubles trying to find ways to get him in. There was a Reverend Eugene Murphy who was a missionary to Japan many, many years ago, was connected with our congregational church, Japanese congregational church. And so he pulled some strings. I don't know how he did it, but he made it possible for us to send my brother to Firland Sanitarium, which was for tuberculosis patients. So I'm not sure what year it was, but 1936 or something like that, '37, he was able to go out to Firland. Let's see. Maybe I get the years a little bit wrong, but he was pretty weak by the time he was able to go to sanitarium. And so he spent the war years out at the sanitarium because when the, after Pearl Harbor, he couldn't leave the grounds of the sanitarium. He wasn't well enough to get out yet, but during that time, he was well enough, but he had to stay on the grounds. So he was fortunate, because he was there, he got to work with the group that was doing x-rays, and so he learned quite a bit about photography and x-rays. And so he taught my husband some of the, when he came out, he helped my husband do some developing pictures, so it was quite a thing. He helped him make an enlarger and tried enlargements. So that's why there's a lot of pictures that he played with, with my husband.
AD: Do you remember hearing on the news about the bombing of Pearl Harbor?
SE: Oh, yes. I don't remember if I heard it on the radio because in the morning, I was working in a restaurant, and I have to be there early in the morning, I forgot what time it started, maybe seven or eight. But I remember going down on the streetcar down to work. And when I got off the streetcar, there were soldiers milling around all over, it's downtown Jackson Street in Seattle. And there were soldiers in the trucks, and I had to weave my way through them to get, go to work. And these were the same boys that were coming in when they were in town over the weekend, and they would come in and have hamburgers or coffee or Coke. And these were the same fellows. They looked different to me that morning because they had their bayonets and they were all stationed around the restaurant, in fact, in and out. And it was scary because I thought, "Wow, all of a sudden we're enemies," and overnight it changed, the whole atmosphere changed. Even the boys weren't smiling. They spoke curtly to us, and it made you feel very uncomfortable. Got up in the morning and it was a different world. So it was quite scary. And then all day at work we would hear news that so-and-so's father was taken, they picked up so-and-so, and so we were just wondering who's next. Eventually, after a while, the man that owned the restaurant was taken, so his wife had to carry on the business. So it was scary, but we were just as busy, in fact, busier after the war started. But then all of a sudden we had to buy special blinds and tape the blinds at the homes. We couldn't have any light showing. And there was a curfew where... I don't know if it was seven o'clock or eight o'clock that we weren't supposed to be out in the streets. So I can't remember what happened at the restaurant, whether they were able to work. Because they used to be open sometimes all night, or close after midnight.
