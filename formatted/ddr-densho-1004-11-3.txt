gky: How did you wind up leaving Fort Jefferson for...
GH: I'm sorry?
gky: How did you wind up leaving Fort Jefferson for Camp Savage?
GH: From Jefferson, after we were -- I might add that after we were discharged or released from the protective custody, we were all assigned to an individual flight. And I would like to bring this up because it was for the first time I felt like a full-fledged citizen. We were assigned to each barracks, and we were treated as each individual. I do remember one day they asked for a volunteer for general's chauffeur. We all raised our hand. But the next day I saw the volunteer pushing a wheelbarrow. [Laughs] And a couple days later, they asked for someone who could type, and I raised my hand. They said, "You." And I was selected. I said to myself, "I wonder what I'll be doing." But sure enough, I was given a flight clerk's position which I was responsible for coming up with the KP and duty roster. So you never saw my name on it. [Laughs] I then became a plans and training clerk, and that's when I was promoted to corporal. And as a corporal, I was the NCO in charge of a barrack, and I was also responsible for the weekend passes. So I did live it up; I was very fortunate. So the fact that the flight commander did have faith in me and he did ask me to do many of his work, which made me feel pretty good.
gky: Is that when you felt like a citizen, full-class citizen?
GH: Yes, I felt like a full-class citizen because, as a corporal among all recruits, that was a position to be in because they would address you as "sir" and they even saluted you.
gky: How'd you wind up getting recruited for the MIS?
GH: Yes, well, one day we were told at Jefferson Barracks, all theNiseiswere told to assemble in the front of their barracks. And a truck came and we boarded the truck and sent, taken to the train station. There we opened, we each opened our brown bag, and mine gave the destination of Fort Leavenworth, Kansas. And while I was stationed at Fort Leavenworth, Kansas, as a plans and training clerk, I received a letter from Colonel [Kai] Rasmussen asking for my resume, and shortly thereafter I was accepted to the Military Intelligence School at Camp Savage.
gky: Did you have any idea what you would be doing?
GH: No.
gky: Did you know it had to do with the fact that you knew Japanese and you wrote Japanese and was fluent?
GH: Yes. Well, I thought it would involve translation.
gky: Before you were actually assigned anywhere, did you think about the possibility of your fighting against Japan? You lived there and you had relatives there, friends there.
GH: Yes. Right. But this is war. You see, it's very unfortunate, but that's war.
gky: When you think about your days at Camp Savage, you were in the top class in Camp Savage, the first class in Camp Savage as well, right?
GH: Yes.
gky: That was in June of '42 that you were scheduled to graduate.
GH: That's correct.
gky: And did you graduate on time, as opposed to...
GH: No, I graduated in '43.
gky: I'm sorry, I'm sorry.
GH: So we were not the first class to graduate.
gky: When you were going to Camp Savage, can you think of just one story that sort of sums up your time there?
GH: In Camp Savage?
gky: Yeah.
GH: Well, it was very intensive. And I can't recall the exact time, but we got up at 7:00, we had our breakfast and we were in the classroom around 8:00 till 12:00, and then we had lunch. We studied until 4:00 and then we went back again around 7:00 to 8:00 o'clock. And we did have weekly exams on Fridays. You couldn't go to the latrine because all the seats were occupied, people studying, taking advantage of the light. As a matter of fact, they had to put a guard, at one time, to accommodate those who were there for legitimate reasons.
gky: When they taught you at Camp Savage, they were teaching you military terminology and stuff that you normally wouldn't learn in Japanese class.
GH: Right.
gky: But were they also teaching you about the psychological...
GH: Yes, they did. And also how to interrogate. It's somewhat, very comical, but at least they tried. For instance they said, "To you tell the difference between a Chinese and a Japanese is to have them take their shoes off. The Japanese have a spread between the big toe and the second, because they woregetaorzori." [Laughs]
gky: About any psychological stuff they may have -- or cultural stuff, or do they expect you to know that having grown up with parents who were immigrants?
GH: In our case, there were quite a -- the majority of the class in Section 1 wereKibei, so those who had experience in Japan. So we were quite familiar with the Japanese psychology.
