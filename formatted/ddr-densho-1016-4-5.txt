AD: Gene, tell us about what, what were you doing when Pearl Harbor happened? What do you remember?
GA: Well, at that time, I was... I had just turned sixteen, and the gang and I were choosing up to play street basketball when some of the kids came out saying Pearl Harbor was bombed. We could hardly believe that, but, so anyway, we disbanded and we all went home to listen to the radio. And sure enough, Pearl Harbor was bombed. And the thoughts that came to me was, "What's gonna happen to us?" Or, "How's the kids at school gonna treat us?" It wasn't long that I had to wait; the following day we went to school, and there was a little bit of a coolness in the air. But the ones that I knew, they said, "Oh, it's not your fault." So just go along as if nothing had happened.
AD: So after Pearl Harbor, how did things change in terms of how people treated you? Maybe start out with that phrase, by saying "after Pearl Harbor..."
GA: Well, my association after they, they made us quit school, why, it was amongst our, our own group, which is all, basically, Japanese. Therefore, I didn't feel it, much of it. But as we went along there, they had, I guess it was in January, they, all the draftees who were in the services, Japanese Americans that were in the service, they were all taken off the active list, and a lot of 'em were put into 4-C category, or some of whom were put into, like menial jobs in the camp, being a janitor or cook's help or something like that. And the rest of the eligible JapaneseNiseis, who were eligible for the draft, we were all registered as 4-C, which is an "enemy alien."
AD: How did you feel to be classified as an "enemy alien"?
GA: Well, at that time, I was only sixteen as they say, so didn't bother me. And the things that is, about that is that previous year, when I was in my sophomore class, we had a whole year of civics, which is the workings of the democratic government. So I thought nothing of it, and I thought well, things will just go along as if nothing had happened.
AD: Tell us more about that story. I remember you mentioned that in your Densho interview in terms of how you viewed your civics class after Pearl Harbor, that the democracy had...
GA: Oh, yeah, the teacher spent a whole year teaching us democracy, and well, I found out that one stroke of President Roosevelt's pen had wiped all that one year teachings, just wiped it out completely. And I had thoughts of, maybe I should write -- it's an afterthought -- I should have written to the teacher saying, "Boy, what you taught me in a whole year, just gone down the drain." But I never got around to do that. That's being kind of nasty. [Laughs]
AD: Tell us more, though, about what, how did, how did democracy get, get lost specifically?
GA: Well, gosh, freedom, the rights, liberty, the Constitutional rights, name it, everything was taken away from us. So it was just the status of a "enemy alien." Now, if that isn't taking away your democracy, your liberty, I don't know what you'd call it.
