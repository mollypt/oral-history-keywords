MO: I was always with my grandmother, and she was such a hard working person. She worked from dawn to dusk. She was a farmer, and she started when the morning came and kept going until the evening. Everyone was working hard, and I was working with them. I worked with everyone in the morning and went to school. They needed help in the field after I came home from school. I was constantly working. I helped only when I came home from school when I was in elementary school. I was considered as an adult when I got into high school and had to be the first to get up. My aunt had children, and I was the one who was responsible, before the other family members woke up, for getting up before everyone else, cooking rice and makingbentolunch before I left for school. I helped in the field after I came home from school and studied at night when it was dark. That is how it was. I am healthy and tall thanks to the field work. I was full of energy and didn't mind working hard at all. I worked very hard. I did it for myself. I never thought I was doing it for other people. My teacher asked me if I would be interested in taking a test to qualify as an elementary school teacher when I was in the fourth grade in high school. The senior grade was fifth at the school, but I was told fourth graders were also allowed to take the test. So I did. Something funny happened. I took the test on the first day, two or three subjects, language art, math, P.E. and music, and I was feeling pretty confident because they were my favorite. I was not feeling well though and a bit worried. The test result was announced every day to notify who was qualified to take the next test. My uncle went to school to see the result, came home and told me that he didn't see my number on the qualified list. I didn't go to take the test on the second day. I thought I had failed the test and didn't go. The school principal sent a custodian over to find out why I didn't show up. We didn't have telephones in Japan yet in those days. He asked me why I didn't come. I rode my bicycle to school, and they were wondering if I got hurt on the way to school. The school principal contacted me. I was so surprised. It would have been fine if I passed the test. I thought I had failed the test, and it was too embarrassing to go to school. We were laughing. [Laughs] That was a bit strange, and I finished my fourth grade and graduated from high school when the fifth grade was over. I was trying to figure out what I would like to do and decided to get some skills. I went Tokyo to go to a vocational school for sewing and crafts because I liked it, and wanted to gain marketable skills and be financially independent. One of my teachers graduated from the school, and I went to Tokyo to go to the same school. I went to school and found a job. I was doing my best to be independent. That is how I ended up going to school. [Laughs] It was not just because I liked it.
BB: Was your first school big or small when you were a child?
MO: Let's see. There were about sixty students in one grade with two classes with a total of thirty boys and girls in each class, in my elementary school. One class was for students who would go up to the higher education institutes. The class was separated for students who would not take the entrance exam even in elementary school. One class was for those who would continue on to high school, and the other one was for those who would be done with middle school. I think there were about sixty students total.
BB: What was your house like? Did you have a private bath? How was it? Did you have electricity?
MO: We didn't have any of those.
BB: People didn't have it in those days.
MO: Not at all. It was a rural area, and we had a well for water. That was our water supply. We lived in a house in a wooded area in the countryside and used the well for a long time. We had to wait for a long time to get electricity too because we happened to have high voltage power lines by the area. It was much later than when we had our electricity. The technology was not advanced. They were not able to run power lines because we had big ones up. They were blocking local lines to come through. They hadn't figure out how to run underground lines yet. Electricity was so close, but we didn't get it for a long time. Yes.
BB: Did you go to a public bath, or did you have your own bath?
MO: We had a private bath at home.
BB: Who was responsible for that?
MO: It was my duty to prepare the bath. I filled the bathtub and burned wood under it. Modern houses have a bathtub in the house, but old Japanese houses had a separate bathroom. The bathroom was detached from the main house. It was scary to go to the bathroom at night. [Laughs] That's where I grew up.
BB: Did you celebrate New Year's? What did you do for the New Year celebration?
MO: New Year was a bit hard for farmers to celebrate on January 1st like everyone else in the country. January was the planting season forigusastraws in Okayama. You probably don't know whatigusais. Do you knowtatamimats? Planting was done around New Year for Japanesetatamimat straws. We planted them around New Year and harvested them in the middle of the summer. New Year's was a hard season to have a celebration because everyone was busy. We always celebrated the lunar calendar New Year, Chinese New Year when I was a child. We had a couple of days off, prepared a big meal, poundedmochicake and got fed well.Igusastraw production was shrinking when we were growing up, and children went to work somewhere else and started to celebrate the solar calendar New Year because of their work schedule. People are no longer celebrating the lunar calendar New Year. It does not work with the modern schedule. Now they celebrate the New Year just like we do here.
BB: Did you have any other special celebrations?
MO: We hadBonfestival, New Year and spring and autumn equinox just like we are still celebrating here. I went to visit a temple. Not as much as I do here, but I still did. [Laughs]
