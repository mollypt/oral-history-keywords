JA: Tell me about the circumstances of your finally leaving camp and going home.
AM: Okay, well, my father said that he's going to be the last one to leave because he has to do a lot of photographing, so when they started giving temporary leaves and things like that, I came into Los Angeles two or three times looking for a job, and then I left camp about... let's see, the camp closed in November, I left camp about June, around there. I found a job, so I was, I started to work. And then in the meantime, my father decided to come back, so I returned to camp and helped him pack. And we made about three trips on a truck. We went in with two suitcases, we came back three truckloads. The reason for that is because after this Caucasian photographer left the studio, my father said, "I can't be using this kind of equipment," because they were very old and pretty inadequate, so he went to the Evacuee Property and told them the situation. He has a lot of equipment in storage in Los Angeles and he'd like to get some of those equipment back into camp, and the Evacuee Property head thought and thought and got an idea. He wrote to Washington, D.C. headquarters and told them that, "The storage company that my father has all of his equipment in is going bankrupt, so we gotta get everything out of there." My gosh, when everything came, it was so many things. Among them was my bicycle and a couple of tricycles for my brother and sister to play on, and so I was one of the few in Manzanar that had a bicycle. So, after he got all that into the camp, he started using his own equipment, and as a result of that, now, to go to back to Los Angeles it was quite a problem, so we, this friend of mine had a tomato farm, a truck farm in San Pedro, so he asked me if I would go down with him to San Pedro because he still had his truck. So I said sure, you know, I wanted to get out of the camp somehow, so I accompanied him and he got his truck and he came back on the truck, and then with that truck, my father was able to get all of his belongings back into Los Angeles. So...
JA: After three trips.
AM: Yeah, it took three trips. And then of course everybody couldn't get on the truck so my father bought a Model A Ford while he was in camp. The way he got that is kind of unusual. There was a man in Independence who used to come to sell eggs about every two weeks and my mother used to buy the eggs from him, they were very inexpensive, and then when he heard that the camp was going to close, he told my father, "I guess I won't be needing this car anymore, so I guess I'll get rid of it." So my father looked at it and says, "Well, how much you want for it?" So he said, "Oh, $200." He says, "I'll take it." And he bought it, and it was a 1929 Model A Ford four-door sedan. And it had a pretty worn-out main bearing, so the engine was making a lot of noise. My cousin's husband, who used to do hot-rodding before going into camp, he listened to the engine and he says, "Oh boy, this car, you can't go any faster than 35 miles an hour." So in spite of that, my father bought the car, so the three trips on the truck we made, this Model A Ford made the trip through at the same time. So the only time we went fast was going downhill, we could get close to about 60 miles an hour. Outside of that, we had to chug along at about 35 miles an hour.
JA: But there's a fair amount of downhill between Manzanar and L.A.
AM: Yes, uh-huh.
[Interruption]
AM: We did a lot of traveling with that Model A Ford. Towards, near the end of the camp, I was back in camp, returned to camp, and my father got a job to take photograph of arts and crafts of three camps, Manzanar, Poston, and Gila, so he thought it was a good chance to make some money, so he took that job on, and the only way to go there was to go on this Model A Ford. So, boy, there was my father, myself, and another, my cousin's husband who is pretty good at Ford mechanics. So he said, "I'll go along just in case something happens." Well, we had to go at a very slow speed all the time, and then near desert center, all of a sudden the engine stopped right in the middle of the hot desert. I thought, "Oh, my gosh, what's going to happen?" Well, he opened up the hood and looked in there and the engine mount kind of fell apart so the engine fell, and it was resting on the chassis so the pulley wouldn't, couldn't turn anymore. So, one of the first things he did was he opened up the front seat, turned the seat over, and boy, talk about junk, there was all kinds of things in there. People, it looked like, just threw it in there just to get things out of the way, you know. There was all kinds of hardware in there, and the previous owner just left everything in there when he sold the car to my father. It was a good thing. He looked and looked and he found a railroad spike, and then there was a ball-peen hammer in there, so he says, "Maybe I'll try with this," he said, you know. So he got the railroad spike and put it between the engine and the chassis and pounded it in, and the engine slowly came up. And we were able to continue our journey that way. And so we went through Poston and Gila and back to Manzanar and made how many trips to Los Angeles with that, with that, the way the car was, until it was stolen. [Laughs]
JA: Probably a good thing. [Laughs]
AM: Yeah, it was a good thing because --
JA: Your father was very mechanically clever. Did he get paid for photographing the weddings and things like that?
AM: Yes, see, the way it was priced was because the enterprise was a nonprofit organization so whatever income we had, we all -- it had to all be turned into the office, and we got paid the regular wage, like I got $16 a month, my father got $18 because he was head of the photo department, and I was a worker so I got $16 a month. Just like everybody else that worked in other departments in the camp, see, so the pay came from the federal government, I guess. So all the money that, income from the sale of the photograph went to the main office, and so the price was very cheap. Not too much profit in it at all, just enough to keep the studio going.
JA: Did they cover his expenses?
AM: Yes, it covered the expense, yes, all the supplies and things. So, we didn't live off the income from the photo department, the sale of the photos. We, we got paid the regular government pay just like anybody else.
