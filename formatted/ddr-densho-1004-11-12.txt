gky: There has been some -- people, during the war, made choices about what they did. Some did choose to resist, some people stayed in camp, some people just waited for the war to go on by them. But you all took an active role. You know, you were already in the military, but you were sort of forced to take an active role.
GH: Well, anyway, I didn't have to make any decisions. I was already a soldier. I had already taken my oath and I was prepared to serve my country to the best of my ability. I know others did have a very difficult decision to make, especially if you're an American citizen. And I'm sure many said, "Get me out of the camp, free my parents, and I'll go, I'll volunteer, and I appreciate their position." They stood on their constitutional grounds. I respect them, too.
gky: You said others stood on their constitutional grounds. How did you feel about your constitutional rights having been taken away and yet still fighting for your country?
GH: Well, for me, I think it was a little bit easier because I had to a certain extent been brainwashed. I did spend eight years in Japan. I was taught, and which to me sounded reasonable, that you serve one's country to the best of your ability. That was your right as well as responsibility. My country was America. I was in the American army, and I was prepared to do my best.
gky: Was there any question in your mind, when you were living in Japan and there was unrest in Japan, and you were talking to Martin about it, did it ever occur to you to stay in Japan and serve the Japanese army?
GH: No. As a matter of fact, I was offered something comparable to Officers Candidate School, which I declined on the grounds that I was an American.
