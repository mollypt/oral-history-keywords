JD: Where did you first meet Roy Matsumoto?
GH: I met, I first met Roy at Camp Savage. That's the Military Intelligence School. The class was divided into sections, section one, two, three, down to twenty and some odd sections. I was in one. We were in the same building. Section one and two were in the same building. That's where I met Roy.
JD: What was... can you tell us a little more about the training that you and Roy and the rest of the Marauders went through before you were...
GH: At Camp Savage? Yes. Well, it was a very intensive course. The course consisted of translation from Japanese to English. We had interrogation lessons, [inaudible], cursive writing. A lot of military terminology. Interrogation and translation of documents. Also intercepting regular messages. It was so intense that, as I recall, the classes were from eight to five and then from seven to nine. On Saturdays we had the exam in the morning. The instructions were very intense and people, after lights out, which was ten o'clock, they would go to the latrine to study. I recall at one time they had to post a guard because all the seats were occupied and the guard had to be there to accommodate those who were there on legitimate purpose.
JD: It sounds as if it must have just become like a blur, this training and studying.
GH: I'd also heard that people used to study with their flashlight under the blanket. That's how intense it was.
JD: I'm wondering, when did you first hear about the "No-No Boys" and what did you think about their position?
GH: That was after the war. I wasn't aware of it until after the war. Well, I think each person may have acted according to their conscience. When I heard a lot of people talking about the constitutional rights, well, the majority of us didn't know constitutional rights. We were discriminated so long that almost took it for granted. I think there were, I'm sure there were some, like Gordon, who went to college and knew the Constitution, acted accordingly. But I'm sure there were others who took advantage of the... and resisted the draft.
JD: Once the war got going and the U.S. was bombing Japan and so on... Japanese cities, did you all hear anything about that?
GH: Oh yes. After Burma, I was in India and I was assigned to the British Royal Air Force with Roy. Then subsequently I was transferred to Kunming, China, and to Chungking. While I was in Chungking I was very much aware of what was going on as far as bombing and the war condition in Japan, against Japan.
JD: Were you afraid for relatives?
GH: I do recall... we had to take turns having our... we were paid in dollars and in turn we had to convert that into yuan. The official rate was about 250 to 1 whereas the black market was over 1000 to 1. You determined the rate according to the war situation. So I would walk into the war situation to look at where the, how the war was progressing. ANd I would see all the cities to be bombed depicted. Those that were bombed had a red X on it. So it did give me an idea of what was going on.
JD: This was a map of cities in Japan?
GH: Yes. They had a whole wall covered with cities to be bombed. Yeah. One thing that bothered me was Kyoto was on it. And I said, gee, of all places, I'd hate to see Kyoto being bombed. But fortunately Emerson and others did prevail and it was saved.
JD: Did you know they were firebombing cities?
GH: I heard of it, yes.
