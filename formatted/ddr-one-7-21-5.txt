[This transcript is a translation of the original Japanese text.]
BB: Why did you come back to the States with your family in 1955?
MO: I came back on a boat.
BB: On a boat.
MO: Yes.
BB: Why did you come back?
MO: My mother invited me back. She had no intention to go back to Japan. She asked me to come back. I told her that I was doing fine in Japan and asked her if she wanted to come back. She was very stubborn. [Laughs] She didn't want to come back. She offered to cover the travel expenses for the entire family if we were to join her. I was giving it a lot of thought and decided to move to the U.S. I knew it would take some adjustment, but I thought it would be the better place. Soon after we came over, we came over in 1955, she got palsy in 1957. I think it was the right decision to move over here. She suffered from palsy for eighteen years. It would have been very hard if she had been all by herself.
BB: Was that the first time for your husband to be in the States?
MO: Yes, it was his first time.
BB: How did he like it?
MO: I think he had some difficulties. He didn't say anything though. [Laughs]
BB: Did he quickly learn English?
MO: That was the hard part.
BB: Did he work for the tofu store?
MO: Yes. He had a different job for a while, but my mother got sick. She was in bed, and we figured the tofu store would feed the family. She encouraged us to take over the business to support our family. Right after she got sick, right after we came over, we came over in June, and I started to work as a seamstress in White Stag in August. I didn't get involved in the tofu store at all. I was just sewing. [Laughs]
BB: What did you do?
MO: I sewed.
BB: Where did you do the sewing?
MO: In White Stag. I was a trained seamstress and was offered a job there at White Stag right away even I didn't speak English. The first position was piecework. I was sewing. I then became a supervisor and trained other employees even though I didn't have English language skills. I kept working for White Stag for 23 years. When I was ready to retire, the family tofu store was pretty busy. I couldn't run the store or didn't want to. I didn't want to retire from my job and learn how to run the business. [Laughs] My son just graduated, and we talked about his future. His options are to get a job somewhere else and leave, or to take over the family business. It would have been a waste of a good business if we had just thrown it away because it was too busy. We talked to the daughters too, and he decided to take over the family business. I sent him to Japan in around 1970, he graduated from college in 1970, and I sent him over to apprentice with a Japanese tofu maker for a few months. He came back and started to run the business right away.
BB: Did your husband like working for the tofu store?
MO: I don't think he liked it, but he did run the business. [Laughs] He just had to. He had been working at a lab in a hospital. He was just looking into microscope. He was looking into a microscope and conducting lab tests. I felt sorry for him. He did it because he had to. Once a doctor offered him a position, but he didn't have the language skills. Besides, you would need to kill a lot of animals when you work at a lab. You would also have to draw blood for tests. He probably didn't like the idea. We made him into a tofu maker. [Laughs]
BB: How about your son? Is he still running the business?
MO: Yes, he is.
BB: Where is your store located? It used to be in the Japantown, didn't it?
MO: Yes. It is currently located on Stark Street, in the south east side. We built a new store on Stark Street in 1980, and that is where the store is now. It has been more than twenty years since we moved. We are still there.
