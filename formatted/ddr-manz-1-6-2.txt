AM: Well, the problem was... one of the big problem was how to get the film. Well, it so happened that there was a hardware salesman that used to come from Los Angeles and take orders in camp once a month, and he found out that this salesman that used to come take orders was a person that he helped while my father had the studio in Little Tokyo. And this company that this representative, representative was -- this California Hardware, which was a big distributor, which was right in Little Tokyo, and it just so happened that my father used to do a lot of work for this hardware company. And the work got so heavy and it was so busy taking photographs of their merchandise and things so the Hardware decided to put in their own photo department. So my father willingly went over to the Hardware and showed 'em how to make a studio and darkroom and things like that. And the person who was in charge of the photo department was the same salesman -- it just so happened it was the same man that came to Manzanar to take orders. So, he found that out so he asked this salesman if he would do him a favor, he'd like to have this certain size film and type of film, so if he would go to this dealer where my father used to buy all of his supplies, so he said, "Sure, I'll do that for you," you know. So next trip when he came back, he came with his merchandise. But now, how to get it to my father without being caught. So this man had an idea, leaving the thing in his jacket pocket, and told my father that his jacket would be hanging in the hall, hallway of the administration building, so if he could get somebody to come and pick it out of his pocket. So my father knew a lot of the policemen in the police department because they were all Japanese American except for the police chief. So he asked one of the policemen if he would go to the administration building, and there'd be a jacket hanging in the hallway and, "There's something in there for me," so if he would get it for him. So the fellow said, "Sure," so this policeman got in on it and got the thing out of his pocket and delivered to my father, and that's how he was getting his supplies. And then one time my father ordered a chemical and there was no way to put that in a pocket so he told my father, "I'll leave my trunk ajar, so, and I'll give you the license number and the color of the car, so have him pick it out of there." And sure enough, it was there, so that's how he was getting his photographic supply.
And then in the meantime, people in the camp wanted a photography studio because they knew my father was a photographer. There was a Manzanar Cooperative Enterprise, which was a non-profit enterprise, and under that was barber shop, dry goods store, canteen, and shoe repair. And so under that enterprise, my father thought, well, maybe he could have a studio in there. Well, he went to the camp director and he said, "Sure, but you're in the, still in the camp and you're not allowed to take any pictures," so the camp director suggested getting a Caucasian fellow to just click the shutter. Well, found this young person who had just finished photographic study and he was hired to come into camp to be a photographer in camp. And what, my father would do all the posing and lighting and things to get it all ready and this fellow would come and click the shutter. That went on for a while, and one time there was a group of about two or three hundred people, and my father arranged the camera and everything and then the fellow clicked the shutter. And it was about close to five o'clock, and this fellow was in a hurry to get home that day so he inadvertently took the lens off the camera with the still, with the film exposed in there. So that ruined the picture, and my father got so mad at him, he had a hard time lining that many people and then the films are very difficult to get. And he just really chewed him out, so a few days later this fellow quit. So my father thought, "Well, what are we going to do now that he's gone?" so he went to the camp director and talked to Mr. Ralph Merritt, who was the director. And he thought and thought and said, "Okay, what we'll do is I'll get one of the ladies who works here, her husband works for the WRA, and a lot of these wives have nothing to do all day long. So I'll get one of those ladies, and then as long as she's there, you could take the picture yourself provided you give the lens to her when she leaves every day." So my father was real happy with that. He said, "Sure," you know. So that went on for a while, and then these ladies have to, this lady had to sit there for eight hours, there was nothing to do, and so there was, some of them would crochet, some of them would knit. This lady would knit sometimes. But they got too bored so they quit one after another. And then about five or six ladies just quit and left, so the camp director got kind of tired of looking for somebody. So Mr. Merritt told my father, he says, "You know, Toyo, I can't see anything going on on the left side of me," and my father caught on right away, and so from then on my father was on his own. He was able to photograph -- it was all because of Mr. Merritt, that he overlooked the situation like that. So the reason for studio to be established or people wanting a studio was because some of their sons or daughters, when they graduate from high school, they want to relocate or some of them are volunteering into the army or getting drafted, and there was a, really a need for photograph to be taken so, because if they go overseas, they may never see their son again. So there was really a call for a photo studio, so that was one of the reasons why it was, it was put up, too.
