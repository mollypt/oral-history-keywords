gky: Can you remember the first prisoner that you interrogated?
GH: I can't recall the first; it was just another soldier.
gky: It was unusually late. You wound up interrogating -- first of all, interrogating, not interpreting. Why was that? Why did you end up interrogating rather than interpret?
GH: Actually, there was no -- there was only one officer, intelligence officer, which was Captain Latham whose mother was Japanese. But he spent time more with General Stilwell, Colonel Stilwell, helping him with the intelligence, and he was the only intelligence officer. So we were very much on our own.
gky: What did it -- what was an interrogation like?
GH: Well, you come in and the prisoner comes in. I do talk to him. I say, "Have you been wounded, or are you sick? Have you received a letter from home lately?" And I would usually break my cigarette and share it with him. And the prisoner would say, "Aren't you going to shoot me? Aren't you going to kill me?" I said "No." And they were taken by surprise, and many POWs would even have tears coming down their cheek, and they were very cooperative. But I do recall a lieutenant, second lieutenant, who was brought in by stretcher. He had attempted to escape and the Indian Gurkha with a bayonet stabbed him in his arm, buttock and his thigh, and he was in very bad shape. As a matter of fact, there was quite a few GIs that surrounded and wanted to start kicking him, so I said, "That's got to stop." I said he could -- "The information I may be able to obtain from him may save your life," and with that, I had the guard clear the POW. But the fact, it was late at night, he was in bad shape, so I said, "Take him to first aid and give him whatever aid you can give him. And then, but I want him back first thing in the morning," which they did bring him back and I started to interrogate him. He said, "You traitor." I said, "Traitor?" I said, "I'm an American soldier." He kept on saying I was a traitor. So I said, "Yes, if we were to cut our vein, I guess the same blood will flow. But you're fighting for your country and I'm fighting for mine. We do have difference in our opinion, and I want to make sure that you know that I'm the interrogator and you're the POW." But he refused to answer my questions. And this went on for a while. And finally, in desperation, I said to the guard, "Take him to the stockade." We did have a stockade which accommodated the enlisted men, but we didn't have one for officers. I says, "Take him to the stockade. Put him in the center of the stockade." When I got there, I think it was Miyazaki who was there and he was saying, telling the enlisted men, he say, "Officer in the Imperial Army, look at him." I then walked by the POW and he pulled my trouser, and he said, "Mr. Interpreter," he says, "I want to be shot." I says, "Shot?" I said, "I have no bullet to spare on you, but we do have a captured sword if you want to demonstratehara kirito your enlisted men, you're welcome to." He says "No, no, no, no." And with that, I walked away. On the second round, he pulled my trouser again and he said, "Mr. Interpreter, I have a change of mind." So I said, "All right."So we took him out of the stockade and I interrogated him. He was very cooperative. He had some very good information. The information -- I say good because the report was taken to Captain Chan. It was a day or two later, I did -- it was the first time I got a feedback. He said, "That was the information we were looking for." So I at least had a verbal feedback. And the other translation is one that was written up in the CBI Roundup.
And the other interrogation is that atomic bomb. I was in charge -- this was after Burma. I was in Chunking, and while I was in Chunking, I was in charge of the Japanese air corps POWs. One day during my interrogation, a first lieutenant who had worked with Dr. [Yoshio] Nishina, who was in charge of the atomic research at Tokyo Imperial University, I learned in the course of interrogation. He pushed a small mesh box in front of me and he said, "We're making a research, a bomb of this size that could destroy a city." I says, "Come on." I says, "Don't pull my leg." And he was very serious. He said. "It is true." I said, "Well, what's the name of the bomb?" He saidgenshibakudanand I said to myself,genshi, atom, I know it's small. And then I tried to follow up, but it all became very technical. But I do recall him saying that the research being done at Tokyo Imperial University was led by Dr. Nishina and that the project was sponsored by the army. He also told me there was another project at Kyoto University sponsored by the navy. And there was one more. I think it was University of Osaka. Now, I was so deeply involved with the technical aspect and the words he used was beyond me. Many times I excused myself and went to look up the dictionary, and the dictionary I had did not have the terms which he referred to. I normally spend one week at the camp and then I would return to Chunking, and then spend one week writing up the report. And when I returned from the camp, there were two officers that just graduated from OCS and they were assigned to INTIC Sino Translation and Interrogation Center. I was introduced to them and so I took the advantage. I said, "You just finished OCS. Have you heard of an atom bomb, a bomb the size of a matchbox that could destroy a city?" Well, they rolled their eyes. They looked at each other and said, "Grant, we just graduated from OCS. We have all the latest information on bombs." He said, "There is no such thing." So I excused myself. On Monday, I started working on the report, and the words and vocabulary which I wrote down, they were not to be found in the dictionary. Moreover, it was much too technical for me to handle and I found myself in a bind. So I said, "I better talk to Colonel Burden," who was in charge. So I did make an appointment with him. And when I walked in, he was very preoccupied with a document. I still remember, he was standing next to a window and he constantly looking at the report. And I made my report. And when I said a bomb the size of a match that could destroy a city, he take note of me and then his eyes fell back on the document which he was reading. And when I finished, he was still looking at the document, so I saluted and I dismissed myself. When the bomb was dropped, I commandeered a jeep and I went up to the camp and I looked for the man. And when I told him what had happened, he said, "Where did they drop the bomb at?" I said, "Hiroshima." And he said, "Oh, my gosh." He covered his face and he said, "The war must be over." I said, "It's not over, but it shouldn't be too long."
gky: Boy, that's scary. Scary that you had that information.
GH: Well, it was hard to believe.
