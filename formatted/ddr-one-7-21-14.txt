[This transcript is a translation of the original Japanese text.]
[Off camera]: Did you listen to the emperor's broadcast when the war ended?
MO: Yes.
[Off camera]: On the radio?
MO: Yes, I did.
[Off camera]: What did you think?
MO: I didn't know what to think. The voice was hard to decipher. I didn't understand what he was saying. I understood that the war was over. We were told to listen to the radio for the emperor's speech. The reception was very poor, and we couldn't really decipher his speech. We knew the war was over, and it was such a relief. "Now we can turn of the lights," we thought. [Laughs] It was strange. I bathed my children in the afternoon, before noon on that day. Farmers in Japan rarely took a bath in the daytime in those days. We heard airplanes coming. I thought, "Oh, no." [Laughs] I heard they came for surveillance. They came over to check on us, but I remember it startled me. We could not decipher the broadcast. Everyone was saying that. It was very hard to understand what he was saying.
BB: How did you feel? It was hard to hear. Did you know it was true?
MO: I knew it was true. I felt relieved to hear the war was over. It was such a grave situation. The entire city of Okayama was burned down. Everything was burned to the ground. The Red Cross Hospital was the only one standing in the middle of the city. It survived because it was a brick building. Everything else was completely gone. My husband was working for the Red Cross Hospital. He wasn't there when the city was burned down or when the war ended. The nurses working in the hospital went to the basement for a temporary shelter when the air raid was on. I might be drifting to another subject here. They took shelter in the basement, but it was getting very hot. Some of the nurses ran out of the basement and stated to help other people. The ones who remained in the basement all died from extreme heat. The nurses who ran out to rescue others saved their own lives too. Everything was burned down. The city of Okayama burned down in June. The war ended in August. It was just two months after, and the entire place looked like after Mount Saint Helens erupted. [Laughs] Everything was gone. I personally didn't see any, but my cousins and school children saw a lot of dead bodies lying around on the streets. They were cleared after the air raid and all gone by the end of the war. They could not be left lying around because it was in the summer. They managed to clear the area.
BB: Did you hear about the internment camps of Japanese Americans during the war?
MO: I heard that my family was sent to one but didn't have any idea what was going on.
BB: Did you find out they were sent to a camp on the news?
MO: Yes. I don't remember how I found out. I heard from my father for the last time before the war, and he wrote that it was going to be the last letter and he was not able offer any support to me. I heard that they were sent to a camp somehow, but I don't even know which camp they were sent to.
BB: Did you hear from them right after the war?
MO: I heard about the camp after the war.
BB: You heard about it from your family. Did you hear about it on the news in Japan too?
MO: I don't think I did.
BB: Most Japanese people did not hear about it.
MO: I think that's right.
BB: I see.
MO: Some people went back to Japan before the war started. I know Aya in Matsushima. The brother went back to Japan by the last exchange boat before the war started. The last boat brought some people back, and we got some updates from them.
BB: You went through a lot.
MO: I did.
BB: Mrs. Ota.
MO: It's been a long life.
BB: You are still doing very well.
MO: I am grateful.
BB: And enjoying your grandchildren and great grandchildren.
MO: I am very lucky.
BB: Thank you very much for your time today.
MO: Thank you very much.
BB: Thank you.
