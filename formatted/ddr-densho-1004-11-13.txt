gky: How do you think being in the Military Intelligence Service changed or shaped your life?
GH: After serving with the MIS, I did feel like a first-class citizen. I was very proud to serve. I think we were able to contribute. I would say that we did accomplish a lot, but rather than mentioning each instance, I'd rather just paraphrase General MacArthur who said no commander in the military history had more information about the enemy before the battle. And I think it was General -- these two officers said our effort has saved countless number of lives and has shortened the war by two years. There is, speaking of our unit, the Merrill's Marauders Association had met in Washington, D.C., and on that occasion we had all photos on display, and among them he had an article which read something to the effect that Merrill's Marauders' secret weapon was theNisei, and he gave credit to us for the success of the Burma campaign. General Merrill said, "I could never have gotten along without them."
gky: At what point did you feel like you were contributing to the war effort?
GH: Well, I was able to obtain numerous, valuable information which was passed on to the G2, and I'm sure that they did act on some of the information I was able to provide.
gky: After the war and after your discharge in 1947, you went back to Japan as part of the war crimes.
GH: Yes. I -- after the discharge, I did teach as an instructor at Fort Snelling and the school moved to Presidio, Monterey. From there I was transferred to SCAP legal section where I was assigned to the war crimes, which is not the A class which was more political. I was assigned to the B and C, which was cases where actually dealt with those who perpetrated the crime, or were responsible for the crime. I did serve as an investigator. I apprehended them and we went to trial. I served as a monitor, court monitor, for the prosecution. And when the war crime trial was over, I served on the parole board. So, I said I made a full circle and it was time for me to come back to take advantage of the GI Bill.
gky: Grant, I have one last question. What does patriotism mean to you?
GH: Patriotism, I think a citizen doing his, fulfilling his right and responsibility.
gky: So, patriotism really is more than -- when you say responsibilities, you're saying there's also in a way, not a burden, but there's something that a person has to do.
GH: Yes. Well, our liberty, freedom and liberty comes at a price, and there are times you have to put your life on the line to protect your liberty and freedom.
gky: Anything else you can think of that you want to add about the MIS, Merrill's Marauders, your civil rights?
GH: Well, as we talk, I do recall and I am constantly reminded that our Constitution is vulnerable. I think we all have to be very vigilant and to protect our liberty and freedom.
gky: And are we successful now in doing that?
GH: I'm sorry?
gky: Are we successful?
GH: We have come a long ways, we have a long ways to go, but it's very encouraging to me that we have come a long ways.
gky: Okay. Anything else?
GH: No. Thank you.
gky: Thank you very much.
